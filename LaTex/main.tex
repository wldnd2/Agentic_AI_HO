\title{Capability-Structured Agentic Control for Joint Conditional Handover
and PDCP Duplication in LEO Satellite Networks}
\author{Jiwoong~Jeon and Hoki~Baek,~\IEEEmembership{Member,~IEEE}%
\thanks{(Corresponding author: Hoki~Baek.)}%
\thanks{The authors are with the School of Computer Science and Engineering,
Kyungpook National University, Daegu 37224, South Korea (e-mail:
jjw000628@knu.ac.kr; neloyou@knu.ac.kr).}}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
In low Earth orbit (LEO) satellite networks, user equipment (UE) must change
its serving satellite frequently. Conditional handover (CHO) and packet data
convergence protocol (PDCP) duplication can protect service continuity, but
they turn mobility into coupled preparation, duplication, and switching
decisions while UEs compete for shared satellite resources under delayed and
noisy telemetry. We formulate the problem as a constrained decentralized
partially observable Markov decision process and develop a centralized-training,
decentralized-execution framework in which recurrent memory, ephemeris-based
lookahead, congestion-price coordination, and primal--dual reliability
regulation are introduced as separate agent capabilities. The control loop does not invoke a language model. All $25$ independently trained
policies achieve a lower outage ratio than a tuned 3GPP-inspired CHO rule in
every evaluation scenario, and the complete model reduces the outage ratio by
$60\%$ under nominal conditions and by $46\%$ under compound stress. Compared
with a memoryless learned policy, it attains a statistically indistinguishable outage ratio with $39\%$ fewer handover executions and a $70\%$ lower ping-pong
ratio. A tuned multi-agent deep Q-network reaches a comparable outage ratio,
whereas the complete model yields the lowest ping-pong ratio and lower across-seed variability. Ablations show that goal regulation produces the largest isolated reduction in handover executions.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Agentic AI, conditional handover, PDCP duplication, LEO satellite
networks, multi-agent reinforcement learning.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Non-terrestrial networks (NTNs) are expected to extend sixth-generation (6G)
connectivity beyond terrestrial infrastructure and support ubiquitous service
availability \cite{giordani2021ntn,3gpp38811,fontanesi2026aisurvey}. Among NTN
platforms, low Earth orbit (LEO) constellations are particularly attractive
because their lower altitude reduces propagation delay and path loss compared
with geostationary systems while retaining wide-area coverage
\cite{giordani2021ntn,juan2022handover}. However, orbital motion continuously
changes satellite visibility and limits the time for which a satellite can
serve a user equipment (UE), forcing even stationary UEs to hand over
frequently \cite{ji2021mobility,juan2022handover}. Consequently, LEO access
requires mobility control that is proactive rather than purely reactive.

Proactive LEO mobility has therefore been studied from several perspectives.
Classical, graph-, and flow-based methods select serving satellites using
remaining visibility time, elevation, link quality, or load
\cite{zhang2021networkflow,juan2022handover}. Location-aware conditional
handover (CHO) and AcceHo exploit predictable satellite motion to prepare or
accelerate handover \cite{lee2023locationcho,wu2024acceho}, while SKYCASTLE
reduces network-layer interruption through distributed satellite anchors
\cite{li2024skycastle}. PMPS instead replaces discrete handover interruption
with predictive multi-path packet scheduling when a terminal can track several
satellites simultaneously \cite{wang2026pmps}. Together, these studies exploit
mobility prediction at the target, path, or network layer, but they do not
jointly control the protocol states that precede and follow a handover.

Those states arise directly from standardized mobility mechanisms.
Multi-connectivity exploits multiple radio paths for redundancy and reliability
\cite{pupiales2021mc,lopez2022empiricalmc,3gpp38821}. Within New Radio (NR),
conditional reconfiguration allows a candidate target to be prepared before
the serving link deteriorates and executed later \cite{3gpp38331}, whereas
PDCP duplication can temporarily send the same packet stream over two
lower-layer legs with explicit activation and deactivation \cite{3gpp38323}.
Together, these mechanisms allow a UE to prepare a candidate, protect traffic
with a second leg, switch when appropriate, and release unnecessary resources.
A handover is therefore no longer a single trigger or target decision, but a
sequence of coupled preparation, duplication, and switching decisions.

Managing this sequence is difficult because each action changes both future
feasible actions and shared-resource usage. Aggressive preparation occupies
scarce control and radio resources, persistent duplication wastes capacity,
and frequent switching increases handover and ping-pong events, whereas late
action exposes the UE to outage as the serving satellite moves away. The
decisions of different UEs are also coupled because they compete for the same
satellite capacity and preparation resources. Moreover, each UE observes link
quality and load only through delayed and noisy telemetry, so the instantaneous
observation may not reveal whether degradation is transient or persistent.
We therefore treat mobility management as a sequential control problem under
partial observability and shared-resource coupling.

Learning-based satellite mobility control has progressed from deep Q-network
(DQN)-based handover decisions \cite{yang2023dqn} to multi-agent schemes that
account for distributed users and satellite load
\cite{liu2024marlho,yang2024fingerprints}. GEMS embeds future satellite
mobility into dual-connectivity configuration \cite{wang2026gems}, while
hierarchical reinforcement learning (RL) separates when to hand over from
which satellite to select \cite{zhao2026hrl}. These methods adapt handover
decisions to dynamic conditions, but their action spaces remain centered on
handover timing or serving-target selection. Joint CHO/PDCP control
additionally requires the policy to track protocol state and coordinate
preparation, duplication, switching, release, and rollback over shared
resources. This motivates a controller with memory, prediction, coordination,
and explicit reliability regulation.

These requirements correspond to capabilities commonly associated with
Agentic AI, including perception, memory, reasoning, goal-directed action,
and multi-agent coordination
\cite{sapkota2026agentic,jiang2026tutorial,zhang2026edgeagentic}.
Advanced deep RL (DRL) provides a practical implementation route for such
sequential agents \cite{zheng2026agenticdrl}. Recent wireless and satellite
designs span large language model (LLM)-aided spectrum sharing, decentralized satellite-resource
agents, task-oriented orchestration, hierarchical LLM--algorithm control,
semantic space--air--ground integrated network (SAGIN) management,
LLM-empowered MAPPO, and agentic multipath transmission
\cite{ni2026llmleo,shen2026aers,xie2026agenticiov,cao2026thinkdo,zhang2026saginagentic,hu2026llmmappo,zhang2026agenticmultipath}.
These works illustrate both LLM-centric and domain-specific realizations of
agentic control. For the mobility
problem considered here, telemetry, ephemeris, and protocol states already
provide structured numerical inputs; an LLM therefore need not be invoked at
each control epoch.

Based on this observation, we develop a multi-agent framework for joint CHO
and PDCP-duplication control in shared-capacity LEO networks. Each UE acts as
an autonomous agent that prepares candidates, activates or deactivates
duplication, commits a switch, releases a candidate, or rolls back, and all
agents are trained using multi-agent proximal policy optimization (MAPPO) with
centralized training and decentralized execution (CTDE). Recurrent memory
summarizes local history, an ephemeris-based planner anticipates candidate
viability, a scalar congestion price conveys shared-resource pressure without
UE-to-UE state/action exchange, and a primal--dual regulator adapts reliability
pressure during training. The resulting M1--M5 model family allows each added
capability to be evaluated separately.

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows.

\begin{itemize}
    \item We formulate joint CHO preparation, PDCP-duplication control, and
    primary-satellite switching in a shared-capacity LEO network as a
    constrained decentralized partially observable Markov decision process.
    The formulation captures delayed/noisy telemetry, time-varying visibility,
    hidden link degradation, preparation contention, duplication overhead, and
    mobility cost.

    \item We develop a capability-structured CTDE-MAPPO framework that
    introduces recurrent memory, ephemeris-based reasoning, and
    resource-aware coordination as distinct capabilities. This structure
    enables capability-wise comparisons and targeted within-policy ablations
    without relying on a monolithic LLM controller.

    \item We introduce satellite congestion prices for lightweight
    decentralized coordination without UE-to-UE state/action exchange and a
    primal--dual reliability regulator that automatically adapts the outage
    penalty instead of using a fixed reliability weight.

    \item We evaluate independently trained capability variants against
    conventional handover/duplication heuristics, a 3GPP-inspired CHO
    baseline, and value- and policy-based multi-agent RL baselines under nominal and compound traffic, capacity, and blockage
    disturbances, together with targeted capability ablations.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{LEO Mobility Management and Conditional Handover}

Satellite motion produces time-varying coverage, short serving intervals, and
frequent handovers \cite{ji2021mobility}, so conventional schemes use
satellite-specific information such as elevation, remaining visibility time,
channel availability, and load. Zhang \emph{et al.} formulate multi-user
handover as a network-flow problem whose edge weights reflect user requests
and satellite service quality \cite{zhang2021networkflow}, and Juan
\emph{et al.} analyze 5G NR handover over LEO networks using predictable
satellite motion and antenna information \cite{juan2022handover}. Proactive
mechanisms further exploit orbital predictability: Lee \emph{et al.} trigger
CHO from the distance to serving and target cell reference locations
\cite{lee2023locationcho}, AcceHo predicts future satellite access to reduce
core-network interaction and handover latency \cite{wu2024acceho}, and
SKYCASTLE uses distributed satellite anchors to reduce interruption at the
network layer \cite{li2024skycastle}. These approaches address when and where
a UE hands over, but do not jointly determine candidate preparation, temporary
PDCP duplication, and the subsequent switch.

\subsection{Multi-Connectivity and PDCP Duplication}

Multi-connectivity (MC) uses radio resources from multiple access points for
aggregation or path redundancy \cite{pupiales2021mc}, and simultaneous
terrestrial 5G and Starlink connectivity has been shown to reduce high-end
latency and improve service availability \cite{lopez2022empiricalmc}. 3GPP
considers MC and service continuity for NTN \cite{3gpp38821}, conditional
reconfiguration stores candidate configurations for later execution
\cite{3gpp38331}, and PDCP supports duplication with explicit activation and
deactivation \cite{3gpp38323}, but these specifications do not prescribe a
joint policy for when to prepare, duplicate, switch, and release. GEMS
formulates terrestrial--NTN and NTN--NTN dual connectivity as a temporally
extended matching problem that accounts for future satellite mobility
\cite{wang2026gems}, whereas PMPS assumes terminals that track several
satellites simultaneously and replaces the single-path handover by predictive
multi-path packet scheduling with selective redundancy \cite{wang2026pmps}.
GEMS thus addresses connectivity association and PMPS continuous packet
migration; our work instead retains the standardized CHO and PDCP mechanisms
and jointly controls their lifecycle---candidate preparation, temporary
duplication, switching, release, and rollback---under shared preparation and
radio resources.

\subsection{Learning-Based Satellite Mobility Control}

RL has been applied to adaptive satellite mobility management. DQN-ALrM
combines multiple mobility-related metrics in a DQN-based handover policy
\cite{yang2023dqn}, while multi-agent DRL accounts for dynamic propagation,
user-rate requirements, and satellite load \cite{liu2024marlho}. Yang
\emph{et al.} formulate handover as a decentralized decision process and use
multi-agent fingerprints with double DQN (DDQN) to mitigate non-stationarity
and load imbalance \cite{yang2024fingerprints}. Zhao \emph{et al.} instead
separate handover timing and target selection using a recurrent PPO agent and
a DQN agent \cite{zhao2026hrl}. These methods model handover primarily as a
trigger or serving-target decision. A CHO/PDCP procedure additionally has an
internal state in which preparation, duplication, switching, release, and
rollback constrain future feasible actions and consume resources over multiple
epochs; our framework explicitly models this stateful evolution under
multi-user coupling and partial observability.

\subsection{Agentic AI for Wireless and Satellite Networks}

Agentic AI is commonly associated with contextual perception, persistent
memory, reasoning, goal-directed behavior, autonomous action, and multi-agent
coordination \cite{sapkota2026agentic,zhang2026edgeagentic}; the recent JSAC
tutorial organizes future intelligent communications around closed-loop
perception, reasoning, planning, tools, and memory \cite{jiang2026tutorial},
and advanced DRL has been identified as a key mechanism for sequential agent
decisions \cite{zheng2026agenticdrl}. LLM-centric designs use language models
to interpret quality-of-service (QoS) or operator intent, to reason at a high level, or to guide
lower-level learning, e.g., for LLM-aided spectrum sharing in LEO networks
\cite{ni2026llmleo}, in the Think-Do separation of LLM reasoning from real-time
execution \cite{cao2026thinkdo}, in a monitor--analyze--plan--execute
(MAPE-K) SAGIN architecture in which LLM agents cooperate with an RL learner
\cite{zhang2026saginagentic}, and in LLM-guided MAPPO for satellite--terrestrial
power and rate allocation \cite{hu2026llmmappo}. Domain-specific designs
realize agentic behavior without an LLM in every control decision: AERS uses
decentralized beam agents for satellite resource scheduling
\cite{shen2026aers}, task-oriented agents orchestrate resources in
satellite-assisted Internet of Vehicles (IoV) \cite{xie2026agenticiov}, and
distributed agents construct intent-aware routes combined with multipath
congestion control in LEO networks \cite{zhang2026agenticmultipath}. Our
controller follows the domain-specific direction but addresses a different
control layer: recurrent state retains temporal evidence, ephemeris lookahead
evaluates future mobility, congestion prices summarize shared-resource
pressure, and a dual variable regulates reliability during training. These
capabilities jointly govern the CHO/PDCP mobility procedure rather than
spectrum, beam, routing, or power allocation, and the execution-time control
remains numerical without an LLM.

\section{System Model}
\label{sec:system}

We consider the downlink of an NTN in which a set $\Lset=\{1,\dots,L\}$ of
LEO satellites serves a set $\Nset=\{1,\dots,N\}$ of ground UEs. Time is
slotted into control epochs $t\in\{0,1,\dots,T-1\}$ of duration $\Delta$,
and mobility decisions are taken once per epoch. Each UE is an autonomous
decision-making agent that controls its own CHO and PDCP-duplication
procedure, while all UEs share the finite radio and control-plane resources
of the satellites. The main notations are summarized in
Table~\ref{tab:notation} and the parameter values in Table~\ref{tab:params}.
Throughout the paper, calligraphic letters denote sets and bold letters
denote vectors; a tilde marks a quantity observed by the agents through
delayed and noisy telemetry, a hat marks a quantity predicted by the
analytical planner of Section~\ref{sec:method}, and an overbar marks an
average.

\emph{Modeling scope.} The CHO procedure is represented by a system-level state
machine that abstracts preparation, execution, completion, and cooldown, and
PDCP duplication by the synchronized transmission of the same packet stream
over the serving and the prepared target legs, following
3GPP TS~38.331 and TS~38.323~\cite{3gpp38331,3gpp38323}. Individual RRC
messages, RLC entities, HARQ processes, and radio-link-failure recovery are not
modeled, and the radio scheduler is fixed, so that the agents control only
preparation, duplication, and switching.

Satellite $\ell$ moves on a circular orbit of altitude $h$ and inclination
$\iota$. Its Earth-centered position is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}_\ell(t) = (E+h)\big[\cos\Omega_\ell(t),\,\sin\Omega_\ell(t)\cos\iota,\,\sin\Omega_\ell(t)\sin\iota\big]^{\mathsf T},
\label{eq:orbit}
\end{equation}
where $E$ is the Earth radius and
$\Omega_\ell(t)=\Omega_\ell(0)\pm2\pi t\Delta/T_{\mathrm{orb}}$ is the orbital
phase for the orbital period $T_{\mathrm{orb}}$. The initial phases
$\Omega_\ell(0)$ are evenly spaced over $[-0.38,0.38]$\,rad, and the sign is
positive for $\Omega_\ell(0)\le0$ and negative otherwise. This construction
represents a stylized local constellation slice with overlapping satellite
passes rather than a full Walker constellation. It creates repeated rise--set
transitions and simultaneous handover opportunities while keeping the
mobility-control experiment computationally tractable. The UEs are fixed on
the Earth surface, with latitudes and longitudes drawn uniformly within a
$\pm0.04$\,rad $\times$ $\pm0.05$\,rad area. The elevation angle
$\epsilon_{i\ell}(t)$ and the slant range $d_{i\ell}(t)$ follow from the
positions of satellite $\ell$ and UE $i$, and satellite $\ell$ is visible to
UE $i$ if $v_{i\ell}(t)=\ind\{\epsilon_{i\ell}(t)\ge\epsilon_{\min}\}=1$.

The received power of the link $(i,\ell)$ in dBm is
\begin{multline}
P_{i\ell}(t) = P_{\mathrm{T}} + G_{\mathrm{T}} + G_{\mathrm{R}}
 - \mathrm{FSPL}\big(d_{i\ell}(t)\big) - A_{\mathrm{atm}} - A_{\mathrm{impl}} \\
 - A_{\mathrm{el}}\big(\epsilon_{i\ell}(t)\big) - \chi_{i\ell}(t) - B_{i\ell}(t) - Z_{i\ell}(t),
\label{eq:rx}
\end{multline}
where $P_{\mathrm{T}}$ is the transmit power, $G_{\mathrm{T}}$ and
$G_{\mathrm{R}}$ are the transmit and receive antenna gains,
$\mathrm{FSPL}(d)=20\log_{10}d+20\log_{10}f-147.55$\,dB is the free-space
path loss for the distance $d$ in meters and the carrier frequency $f$ in
hertz, $A_{\mathrm{atm}}$ and $A_{\mathrm{impl}}$ are the atmospheric and
implementation losses, and
$A_{\mathrm{el}}(\epsilon)=1.5/\max\{0.2,\sin\epsilon\}$\,dB is the
additional low-elevation loss. The shadowing $\chi_{i\ell}(t)$ is a
first-order autoregressive (AR(1)) process with correlation coefficient
$\rho_\chi$ and standard deviation $\sigma_\chi$, $B_{i\ell}(t)$ is the extra
loss caused by blockage events, and $Z_{i\ell}(t)$ is the loss of a hidden
link-degradation regime described below. With the receiver noise
power $P_{\mathrm{N}}=-174+10\log_{10}W+F$\,dBm for the bandwidth $W$ and the
noise figure $F$, and a fixed aggregate interference floor $P_{\mathrm{I}}$,
the signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio (SINR) of a visible link in dB is
\begin{equation}
\gamma_{i\ell}(t)=P_{i\ell}(t)-10\log_{10}\!\big(10^{P_{\mathrm{N}}/10}+10^{P_{\mathrm{I}}/10}\big).
\label{eq:sinr}
\end{equation}
The link is abstracted by a capped Shannon efficiency and
a smooth packet-success model,
\begin{align}
e_{i\ell}(t) &= \min\big\{e_{\max},\ 0.75\log_2\!\big(1+10^{\gamma_{i\ell}(t)/10}\big)\big\},\\
q_{i\ell}(t) &= \Big(1+\exp\!\big(-0.75\,(\gamma_{i\ell}(t)+2)\big)\Big)^{-1}\,v_{i\ell}(t),
\label{eq:bler}
\end{align}
where $e_{\max}$ is the spectral-efficiency cap and $q_{i\ell}$ is the
per-packet success probability, which equals $0.5$ at $-2$\,dB.

UE $i$ has an offered traffic rate $\lambda_i(t)$ in bit/s, and the number of
packets of $S$ bits arriving in epoch $t$ is Poisson distributed with mean
$\lambda_i(t)\Delta/S$. The nominal rate of each UE is drawn uniformly within $\pm35\%$
of the mean rate $\bar\lambda$, and it is multiplied by a burst factor during
traffic-burst events. Packets wait in a finite queue of length
$Q_i(t)\le Q_{\max}$ and are dropped when their age exceeds the deadline
$T_{\mathrm{dl}}$. A UE is active if $Q_i(t)>0$, and its demand is
\begin{equation}
D_i(t)=\min\big\{\lambda_i(t),\ Q_i(t)S/\Delta\big\}.
\end{equation}

Each UE has a serving satellite $\ell_i(t)$ and, during a mobility procedure,
a target satellite $c_i(t)$. A UE requests a data leg on $\ell_i(t)$ and, when
duplicating, a second leg on $c_i(t)$. The satellites run a fixed
demand-proportional scheduler that is \emph{not} controlled by the agents.
For a leg $(i,\ell)$, the requested resource fraction is
\begin{equation}
x_{i\ell}(t)=\min\Big\{1,\ \frac{D_i(t)}{W\,e_{i\ell}(t)\,\kappa_\ell(t)}\Big\},
\end{equation}
where $\kappa_\ell(t)$ is the capacity scale of satellite $\ell$; it equals
the nominal value $\kappa_0$ except during capacity-drop events, in which it
is reduced by a factor (and never falls below $0.05$). We set $x_{i\ell}(t)=0$
if UE $i$ has no leg on satellite $\ell$. Let $n_\ell(t)$ be the number of
UEs holding a preparation reservation on satellite $\ell$; each reservation
blocks a fraction $\varpi$ of the satellite resource, which leaves the
fraction $1-\varpi n_\ell(t)$ for data. The scheduler grants every request
when the total request fits into this fraction and scales all requests
proportionally otherwise, which yields the leg rate
\begin{equation}
R_{i\ell}(t)=W e_{i\ell}(t)\kappa_\ell(t)\,x_{i\ell}(t)\min\Big\{1,\ \frac{1-\varpi n_\ell(t)}{\sum_{j\in\Nset}x_{j\ell}(t)}\Big\}.
\end{equation}
The requested pressure of satellite $\ell$ is
$\Phi_\ell(t)=\varpi n_\ell(t)+\sum_{i\in\Nset}x_{i\ell}(t)$. The scheduler
treats all UEs equally and is kept outside the learned action space, so that
performance differences stem from mobility decisions rather than scheduling.

\emph{Delivery.} Without duplication, UE $i$ transmits at the rate
$R_i=R_{i\ell_i}$ with the success probability $q_i=q_{i\ell_i}$. With PDCP
duplication, the same packets are sent on both legs, so that
$R_i=\min\{R_{i\ell_i},R_{ic_i}\}$ and
\begin{equation}
q_i=(1-\varkappa)\big[1-(1-q_{i\ell_i})(1-q_{ic_i})\big]+\varkappa\max\{q_{i\ell_i},q_{ic_i}\},
\label{eq:dup}
\end{equation}
where $\varkappa\in[0,1]$ is a common-mode dependence parameter. The number of
delivered packets is
$Y_i(t)\sim\mathrm{Bin}\big(\min\{\lfloor R_i(t)\Delta/S\rfloor,Q_i(t)\},\,q_i(t)\big)$.

\emph{Service metrics.} The normalized service deficit and the outage
indicator of an active UE are
\begin{gather}
\delta_i(t)=\frac{\big[D_i(t)-\min\{R_i(t),Q_i(t)S/\Delta\}\,q_i(t)\big]^+}{D_i(t)},\notag\\
u_i(t)=\ind\big\{Y_i(t)S<\beta D_i(t)\Delta\big\},
\label{eq:service}
\end{gather}
where $\beta$ is the minimum acceptable service ratio. Note that $\delta_i$ is
a smooth expected-value quantity used for learning, whereas $u_i$ is the
realized, packet-level reliability event.

Each UE runs the five-state mobility state machine of Fig.~\ref{fig:fsm}:
\textsc{Single} (\textsf{S}), \textsc{Prepared} (\textsf{P}),
\textsc{Duplicating} (\textsf{D}), \textsc{Switching} (\textsf{W}), and
\textsc{Cooldown} (\textsf{C}). The candidate set $\mathcal{K}_i(t)$ contains
the $K$ visible non-serving satellites with the highest \emph{observed} SINR
\eqref{eq:telemetry}. The per-UE action set is
\begin{multline}
\Aset=\{\textsc{Stay},\,\textsc{Prep}_1,\dots,\textsc{Prep}_K,\,\textsc{DupOn},\\
\textsc{DupOff},\,\textsc{Commit},\,\textsc{Release},\,\textsc{Rollback}\},
\end{multline}
which contains $K+6$ actions. A state-dependent hard mask
$\mathbf{b}_i(t)\in\{0,1\}^{K+6}$ enforces protocol and physical validity:
\begin{itemize}
\item In \textsf{S}, $\textsc{Prep}_k$ requests CHO preparation towards the
$k$-th candidate. If admitted, the UE enters \textsf{P} with the preparation
timer $T_{\mathrm{prep}}$.
\item In \textsf{P} (after the timer expires), $\textsc{DupOn}$ activates
duplication (\textsf{P}$\to$\textsf{D}), $\textsc{Commit}$ executes the CHO
(\textsf{P}$\to$\textsf{W}), and $\textsc{Release}$ cancels the preparation
(\textsf{P}$\to$\textsf{S}).
\item In \textsf{D}, $\textsc{DupOff}$ returns to \textsf{P}, $\textsc{Commit}$
executes the CHO, and $\textsc{Rollback}$ cancels both the duplication and the
preparation.
\item $\textsc{Commit}$ is feasible only if the target is visible and the
dwell condition $t-t_i\ge T_{\mathrm{dwell}}$ holds, where $t_i$ is the epoch
of the last completed switch of UE $i$.
\end{itemize}
If the target becomes invisible in \textsf{P} or \textsf{D}, the
preparation is lost and the UE returns to \textsf{S}. In \textsf{W}, the
source satellite remains the serving data leg and the target reservation is
retained until the switching outcome is resolved.

After $T_{\mathrm{sw}}$ epochs in \textsf{W}, the switch succeeds with the
probability
\begin{equation}
\alpha_i(t)=\alpha_0\,q_{ic_i}(t)\,\exp\!\big(-[\Phi_{c_i}(t-1)-1]^+\big),
\label{eq:hosuccess}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_0$ is the base success probability and $\Phi_{c_i}(t-1)$ is the
most recently realized requested pressure of the target, since the outcome is
resolved before the scheduler runs in epoch $t$.

On success, $c_i$ becomes the new serving satellite and the UE enters
\textsf{C} for $T_{\mathrm{cd}}$ epochs; otherwise it returns to \textsf{S} on
the old serving satellite. A successful switch back to the previous serving
satellite within $T_{\mathrm{pp}}$ epochs is counted as a ping-pong.

\emph{Preparation admission.} CHO preparation consumes two separate resources
of the target satellite: the data-plane reservation $\varpi$ and one of $M$
control-plane preparation slots. All preparation requests of an epoch are
collected first. Let $m_\ell(t)$ be the number of new requests towards
satellite $\ell$; if $m_\ell(t)$ exceeds
$\min\{M-n_\ell(t),\ \lfloor(1-\varpi n_\ell(t))/\varpi\rfloor\}$, the
admitted requests are drawn uniformly at random and the others are rejected.
Admission is therefore an explicit multi-UE contention.

The agents do not observe the complete physical state. Current geometric link
availability is assumed to be known for the serving-link visibility indicator
and candidate presence, whereas radio-quality, elevation, and load information
provided to the actor is delayed by $T_{\mathrm{obs}}$ epochs. In addition,
the observed SINR and load are corrupted by temporally correlated noise.
Specifically,
\begin{gather}
\tilde\gamma_{i\ell}(t)=\gamma_{i\ell}(t-T_{\mathrm{obs}})+\nu_{i\ell}(t),
\label{eq:telemetry}\\
\tilde e_{i\ell}(t)=\min\!\Big\{e_{\max},\max\!\big\{0,\,e_{i\ell}(t-T_{\mathrm{obs}})\,2^{\nu_{i\ell}(t)/10}\big\}\Big\},\notag\\
\tilde\epsilon_{i\ell}(t)=\epsilon_{i\ell}(t-T_{\mathrm{obs}}),\notag\\
\tilde\Phi_{i\ell}(t)=\big[\Phi_\ell(t-1-T_{\mathrm{obs}})+\omega_{i\ell}(t)\big]^+,\notag
\end{gather}
where the telemetry noises $\nu_{i\ell}(t)$ and $\omega_{i\ell}(t)$ are
independent zero-mean AR(1) processes with correlation coefficients
$\rho_\nu$ and $\rho_\omega$ and standard deviations $\sigma_\nu$ and
$\sigma_\omega$, respectively. Thus, elevation telemetry is delayed but is not
subjected to an additional measurement-noise term in the present
implementation. The local observation $\mathbf{o}_i(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{16+7K}$
contains (i) the queue length, head-of-line delay, arrival rate,
reliability-class indicator, duplication flag, one-hot protocol state, and
protocol timer; (ii) for the serving satellite: visibility, $\tilde\gamma$,
$\tilde e$, $\tilde\epsilon$, and $\tilde\Phi$; and (iii) for each candidate:
presence, a prepared-target flag, $\tilde\gamma$, $\tilde e$,
$\tilde\epsilon$, $\tilde\Phi$, and the observed SINR trend
$\tilde\gamma(t)-\tilde\gamma(t-1)$. Agents do \emph{not} observe the
remaining visibility time, the capacity scales, the event realizations, the
latent link regimes, or the states and actions of other UEs, and the action
mask does not check whether a candidate remains visible long enough to
complete a handover. The reliability-class indicator is fixed to $0.99$ and is
distinct from the outage threshold $C_{\mathrm{thr}}$ of
Section~\ref{sec:problem}.

\emph{Per-epoch control sequence.} In each epoch, every UE selects one masked
action from its observation, its protocol state, and its local history. The
environment then resolves protocol timers and switching outcomes, replaces
actions that have become infeasible by \textsc{Stay}, jointly admits the
preparation requests, and serves the data legs, after which hidden regimes,
events, telemetry, and congestion prices are updated. A price computed at the
end of an epoch therefore affects only the next decision, so coordination is
causal and one-epoch delayed.

\emph{Hidden link regimes.} Every UE--satellite path $(i,\ell)$ follows a
hidden semi-Markov regime $\textsc{Good}\to\textsc{Degraded}\to\textsc{Recovering}\to\textsc{Good}$.
A path enters \textsc{Degraded} with a small probability per epoch and with a
larger probability at the start of an episode (Table~\ref{tab:params}), stays
there for a duration drawn uniformly between $8$ and $24$ epochs with an extra
loss $Z_{i\ell}$ drawn uniformly between $7$ and $15$\,dB, and then recovers
over $4$ to $10$ epochs, during which $Z_{i\ell}$ decays linearly to zero.
These regimes are observable only through their delayed, noisy effect on
$\tilde\gamma$, which makes memory useful.

\emph{Disturbance events.} Each training and nominal-evaluation episode
contains five events whose start, duration, severity, affected satellite,
and affected UE subset are sampled from the ranges in Table~\ref{tab:params}:
two blockages (extra loss $B_{i\ell}$ on the selected UE--satellite links),
two capacity drops (reduced capacity scale $\kappa_\ell$ of one satellite),
and one traffic burst (increased arrival rate $\lambda_i$ of a subset of UEs).
For the \emph{compound-stress} evaluation, the randomized training-event
schedule is replaced by a deterministic overlapping disturbance pattern: a
traffic burst with the factor $2.5$ on $50\%$ of the UEs during
$t\in[60,120)$, a capacity drop of satellite $2$ to $45\%$ of its nominal
capacity scale during $t\in[80,130)$, and an $18$-dB blockage affecting all
links of $25\%$ of the UEs during $t\in[100,125)$. The burst and blockage severities lie outside their training ranges, the
blockage affects all satellites of a UE, and the three disturbances overlap in
time, which creates an operating condition not encountered during training.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Per-Epoch Costs}

Let $\Nset(t)\subseteq\Nset$ be the set of UEs that are active in epoch $t$.
We define the service, control, and reliability costs of epoch $t$ as
\begin{align}
C_{\mathrm{def}}(t) &= \frac{1}{|\Nset(t)|}\sum_{i\in\Nset(t)}\delta_i(t),\\
C_{\mathrm{prep}}(t) &= \frac{\varpi}{L}\sum_{\ell\in\Lset}n_\ell(t),\\
C_{\mathrm{HO}}(t) &= \frac{1}{|\Nset(t)|}\sum_{i\in\Nset(t)}\ind\{a_i(t)=\textsc{Commit}\},\\
C_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(t) &= C_{\mathrm{dup}}(t)+w_{\mathrm{prep}}C_{\mathrm{prep}}(t)+w_{\mathrm{HO}}C_{\mathrm{HO}}(t),\\
C_{\mathrm{out}}(t) &= \frac{1}{|\Nset(t)|}\sum_{i\in\Nset(t)}u_i(t),
\end{align}
where the duplication cost
$C_{\mathrm{dup}}(t)=B_{\mathrm{dup}}(t)/\big(B_{\mathrm{use}}(t)+B_{\mathrm{dup}}(t)\big)$
is the fraction of the scheduled bits that are duplicate copies
$B_{\mathrm{dup}}$ on secondary legs, $B_{\mathrm{use}}$ being the bits
scheduled on the serving legs. The control cost
$C_{\mathrm{ctrl}}$ thus combines transmission redundancy, preparation
reservations, and handover executions with the weights $w_{\mathrm{prep}}$ and
$w_{\mathrm{HO}}$; it does not charge preparation signaling messages
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Constrained Decentralized POMDP}

The problem is a cooperative, constrained decentralized partially observable
Markov decision process (Dec-POMDP) with the agent set $\Nset$, the state
space $\mathcal{S}$, the action set $\Aset$ of every agent, the transition
kernel $\mathcal{P}$, the team reward $r$, and the discount factor $\xi$. The
state $s(t)\in\mathcal{S}$ comprises the satellite geometry, channel and shadowing states, hidden
regimes and their timers, disturbance events and their remaining durations,
the queues including packet ages, and the protocol states, timers, and
last-switch epochs of all UEs; $\mathcal{P}$ is given by Section~\ref{sec:system}; and
each agent receives the local observation $\mathbf{o}_i(t)$ of
Section~\ref{sec:system} together with its action mask. All agents share the team
reward
\begin{equation}
r(t) = -C_{\mathrm{def}}(t) - w\,C_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(t),
\label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
where $w$ weights the control cost against the service deficit. Reliability
is imposed as a constraint on the long-run outage cost rather than as a
further reward weight:
\begin{equation}
\max_{\boldsymbol\pi}\ \E_{\boldsymbol\pi}\Big[\sum_{t}\xi^t r(t)\Big]
\ \ \text{s.t.}\ \ \E_{\boldsymbol\pi}\big[C_{\mathrm{out}}(t)\big]\le C_{\mathrm{thr}},
\label{eq:cdecpomdp}
\end{equation}
where $C_{\mathrm{thr}}$ is the outage threshold and
$\boldsymbol\pi=(\pi_1,\dots,\pi_N)$ are decentralized policies that map the
local observation history of each UE to its action. The per-satellite
capacity constraints are enforced by the scheduler and the admission rule and
need not be learned. We address Problem~\eqref{eq:cdecpomdp} through its Lagrangian relaxation
\begin{gather}
\max_{\boldsymbol\pi}\min_{\mu\ge0}\ \E_{\boldsymbol\pi}\Big[\sum_t\xi^t r_\mu(t)\Big],\label{eq:lagrangian}\\
r_\mu(t)=r(t)-\mu\big(C_{\mathrm{out}}(t)-C_{\mathrm{thr}}\big),\notag
\end{gather}
where $\mu$ is the reliability multiplier; Section~\ref{sec:m5} describes the
rollout-level primal--dual procedure used in practice.

Problem \eqref{eq:cdecpomdp} is difficult because of (i) partial
observability with delayed telemetry and hidden link regimes, (ii) delayed
consequences, since a preparation blocks resources before any benefit appears
and a target chosen now may set before the switch completes, and (iii)
coupling, since UEs compete for shared resources and congestion lowers the
handover success probability \eqref{eq:hosuccess}.

\section{Proposed Capability-Structured Agentic Framework}
\label{sec:method}

We instantiate the above decomposition as five explicit control capabilities
rather than as an LLM-based controller. M1 uses masked local observations, M2
adds recurrent memory, M3 adds ephemeris-based protocol lookahead, M4 adds a
satellite congestion price, and M5 adds a training-time reliability
multiplier. Table~\ref{tab:models} summarizes the corresponding actor inputs
and training objectives, and Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows their interaction.
The M1--M5 sequence is used to isolate the effect of each added capability; it
does not assume that performance improves monotonically. All variants use the
same base reward except M5, which uses the reliability Lagrangian. Execution
remains decentralized and numerical, with satellites broadcasting only scalar
congestion prices.

\subsection{Perception and Core Decision Making (M1)}

All UEs share one stochastic actor $\pi_\theta$ with parameters $\theta$ and
are trained with centralized training and decentralized execution (CTDE)
using multi-agent proximal policy optimization (MAPPO)~\cite{yu2022mappo}. The
actor maps its input $\mathbf{z}_i(t)$ to one logit per action through a
neural network $\mathrm{NN}_\theta$, and invalid actions are removed by a
masked softmax,
\begin{equation}
\pi_\theta(a\mid\mathbf{z}_i,\mathbf{b}_i)=\frac{b_{i,a}\exp\big([\mathrm{NN}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_i)]_a\big)}{\mathbf{b}_i^{\mathsf T}\exp\big(\mathrm{NN}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_i)\big)},
\end{equation}
where $b_{i,a}$ is the entry of $\mathbf{b}_i$ for action $a$ and the
exponential in the denominator is applied elementwise. Perception in M1 is
the masked, telemetry-based observation $\mathbf{z}_i=\mathbf{o}_i$. The
centralized critic $V_\psi$ with parameters $\psi$ is used only during
training. Its input $\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{c}}(t)$ concatenates the observations of all UEs
with the true per-satellite utilization and pressure $\Phi_\ell$, and, when
available, the satellite prices and the reliability multiplier.

\subsection{Memory: Inferring Hidden Regimes (M2)}

Because link regimes are hidden and telemetry is delayed, the current
observation is not a sufficient statistic. M2 equips every agent with a gated
recurrent unit (GRU) that summarizes its interaction history in the recurrent
memory
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{g}_i(t)=\mathrm{GRU}_\theta\big(\mathbf{g}_i(t-1),[\mathbf{o}_i(t),\mathrm{onehot}(a_i(t-1)),\boldsymbol\zeta_i(t-1)]\big),
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol\zeta_i\in\mathbb{R}^6$ is the agent's own \emph{experienced
outcome}: the last handover result ($+1$ success, $-1$ failure, $0$ none), its
outage indicator $u_i$, its deficit $\delta_i$, the duplication gain (the part
of its expected goodput contributed by the secondary leg, normalized by
$D_i$), its resource-usage indicator, and the experienced congestion
$\Phi/(1+\Phi)$ of its serving or target satellite. The actor input becomes $\mathbf{z}_i=[\mathbf{o}_i,\mathbf{g}_i]$, so that
the actor can condition its decision on recent local outcomes rather than on
the current delayed observation alone. The GRU is trained end-to-end with truncated backpropagation through
time.

\subsection{Reasoning: Protocol-Aware Ephemeris Lookahead (M3)}

The action mask enforces immediate feasibility but does not account for
whether a target will remain visible long enough to complete the procedure,
and the benefit of a preparation appears only several epochs later. M3
therefore adds an analytical lookahead based on the predictable satellite
ephemeris. For every feasible
first action $a$, the planner evaluates a small library $\mathcal{Q}_i(a)$ of
protocol-consistent plans, i.e., action sequences
$\mathbf{a}=(a(0),\dots,a(\tau_{\max}-1))$ of length $\tau_{\max}$ that start
with $a$ (e.g., $\textsc{Prep}_k$--wait--$\textsc{Commit}$;
$\textsc{Prep}_k$--wait--$\textsc{DupOn}$--$\textsc{Commit}$; $\textsc{Stay}$)
and are padded with $\textsc{Stay}$, by rolling the UE's own protocol state
machine forward. For tractability, this hypothetical rollout propagates
protocol timers deterministically. Once a hypothetical \textsc{Commit}
satisfies the dwell and predicted-visibility conditions, completion of the
corresponding switch is treated as successful in the lookahead. Consequently,
the stochastic handover-success model in \eqref{eq:hosuccess} is realized by
the environment but is not explicitly sampled or averaged inside the
analytical planner.

The SINR of link $(i,\ell)$ at the lookahead step
$\tau\in\{0,\dots,\tau_{\max}-1\}$ is predicted from the \emph{observed} SINR
and the ephemeris-predicted range,
\begin{equation}
\hat\gamma_{i\ell}(t+\tau)=\tilde\gamma_{i\ell}(t)-20\log_{10}\frac{d_{i\ell}(t+\tau)}{d_{i\ell}(t)},
\end{equation}
and the link is treated as unavailable once $\epsilon_{i\ell}(t+\tau)<\epsilon_{\min}$.
This heuristic applies the ephemeris-predicted range change from $t$ to
$t+\tau$ to the delayed observation without correcting the observation
delay.
The predicted leg rate uses the nominal capacity scale $\kappa_0$ scaled by the
observed load $\tilde\Phi_{i\ell}$, from which the planner obtains the
predicted deficit $\hat\delta_i(t+\tau\mid\mathbf{a})$ under plan
$\mathbf{a}$, including the duplication diversity of \eqref{eq:dup}. The cost
of a first action is the best discounted plan cost,
\begin{multline}
J_i(a)=\min_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{Q}_i(a)}\sum_{\tau=0}^{\tau_{\max}-1}\xi_{\mathrm{p}}^\tau\Big(\hat\delta_i(t+\tau\mid\mathbf{a})\\
+\mathbf{y}(t)^{\mathsf T}\big[\hat{\mathbf{x}}_i(t+\tau\mid\mathbf{a})-\hat{\mathbf{x}}_i(t+\tau\mid\mathbf{a}_0)\big]^+\Big),
\label{eq:plancost}
\end{multline}
where $\xi_{\mathrm{p}}$ is the planning discount, $\mathbf{a}_0$ is the plan
that stays throughout, and the second term is present only in M4 and M5. Here,
$\mathbf{y}(t)=[y_1(t),\ldots,y_L(t)]^{\mathsf T}$ is the raw price vector,
and $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_i$ is the predicted per-satellite resource footprint of
UE $i$ (serving-leg fraction, secondary-leg fraction while duplicating, and
the reservation $\varpi$ while a target is prepared), so that only the
incremental footprint relative to staying is priced. The control-slot
contention term $m_\ell/M$ of the aggregate demand $U_\ell$
(Section~\ref{sec:coord}) reaches the planner only through the price. The
planner thus ranks plans by predicted service continuity, and in M4 and M5
additionally by recently observed shared-resource pressure; all other costs
are learned through the team reward.

Let $J_{\min}$ and $J_{\max}$ be the minimum and maximum of $J_i(a)$ over the
feasible actions of UE $i$. The reasoning feature of a feasible action is
\begin{equation}
\phi_{i,a}=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{J_i(a)-J_{\min}}{J_{\max}-J_{\min}}, & J_{\max}-J_{\min}>10^{-8},\\[6pt]
0, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:reasoning_feature}
\end{equation}
and $\phi_{i,a}=1$ for masked actions. The vector
$\boldsymbol\phi_i\in[0,1]^{K+6}$ is appended to the actor input:
$\mathbf{z}_i=[\mathbf{o}_i,\mathbf{g}_i,\boldsymbol\phi_i]$. The planner is
\emph{advisory}: the actor learns how much to trust it. It has no access to
future events, hidden regimes, true capacity scales, or the states of other
UEs.

\subsection{Coordination: Congestion Prices (M4)}
\label{sec:coord}

UEs compete for preparation slots, reservations, and data capacity. To
coordinate this coupling without direct UE-to-UE message exchange, M4 uses a
scalar price maintained by each satellite. Let $\mathcal{D}(t)$
be the set of UEs that duplicate in epoch $t$. Satellite $\ell$ measures its
aggregate intended demand
\begin{equation}
U_\ell(t)=\sum_{i:\,\ell_i(t)=\ell}x_{i\ell}(t)+\frac{m_\ell(t)}{M}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}(t):\,c_i(t)=\ell}x_{i\ell}(t),
\end{equation}
i.e., the serving demand, the new preparation requests normalized by the
number of control slots, and the duplication demand, and updates its price
$y_\ell$ by projected subgradient ascent with the step size $\eta_y$,
\begin{equation}
y_\ell(t+1)=\Big[y_\ell(t)+\eta_y\big(U_\ell(t)-1\big)\Big]_0^{y_{\max}}.
\label{eq:price}
\end{equation}
Prices rise while intended demand exceeds the normalized resource level and
decay otherwise; they are reset at the start of each episode and are not
learned. The price produced at the end of epoch $t$ is used only for the next
decentralized decision, so coordination is causal and one-epoch delayed.

The actor of UE $i$ receives the normalized local price vector
$\mathbf{y}_i(t)/y_{\max}$ with
$\mathbf{y}_i(t)=[y_\ell(t)]_{\ell\in\{\ell_i(t)\}\cup\mathcal{K}_i(t)}$,
whereas the planner uses the raw prices. The coordinator never allocates
actions: the only coordination signal is one scalar per satellite and epoch,
which reveals neither individual UE states nor actions and conveys only the
recent aggregate resource pressure.

\subsection{Goal Regulation: Primal--Dual Reliability Control (M5)}
\label{sec:m5}

M5 addresses the Lagrangian problem~\eqref{eq:lagrangian}. The dual update
uses the realized outage fraction over all active-UE opportunities in one
rollout. For the set $\mathcal{T}$ of the $T_{\mathrm{roll}}$ epochs of a
rollout, the implementation computes the rollout-average outage cost
\begin{equation}
\bar C_{\mathrm{out}}=
\frac{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\sum_{i\in\Nset(t)}u_i(t)}{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}|\Nset(t)|}.
\end{equation}
After every rollout, the multiplier is updated by the projected step
\begin{equation}
\mu\leftarrow\Big[\mu+\eta_\mu\big(\bar C_{\mathrm{out}}-C_{\mathrm{thr}}\big)\Big]_0^{\mu_{\max}},
\label{eq:dual}
\end{equation}
and the policy is trained on $r_\mu$. Because $\bar C_{\mathrm{out}}$ is a
rollout-level sample estimate of the constraint in \eqref{eq:cdecpomdp}, this
procedure is a practical primal--dual surrogate of \eqref{eq:lagrangian}
rather than an exact solution. The multiplier is fixed within each rollout and frozen after training, does not modify the planner, and enters the
decision through the Lagrangian reward and the feature $\mu/\mu_{\max}$ of the
actor and the critic.

\subsection{Training Procedure and Complexity}

In each training iteration, all UEs collect a rollout of $T_{\mathrm{roll}}$
epochs with the current policy; the shared actor, the GRU, and the centralized
critic are then updated with the clipped PPO objective~\cite{schulman2017ppo},
generalized advantage estimation~\cite{schulman2016gae}, and truncated
backpropagation through time on the team reward ($r$ or $r_\mu$), with early
stopping at a target KL divergence and linearly annealed learning rates, and
M5 finally updates $\mu$ by \eqref{eq:dual}.

\emph{Execution complexity.} Per epoch, a UE performs one recurrent update
and one actor forward pass, and in M3--M5 the planner evaluates at most
$\max\{2K+1,4\}$ plans over $\tau_{\max}$ epochs, i.e., $\mathcal{O}(K\tau_{\max})$
per UE independently of $N$. On a single thread of an Intel Core i7-12700 CPU
with an unoptimized Python/NumPy implementation (no GPU), the mean decision
latency for all $N=12$ UEs is $0.24$--$0.44$\,ms for the actor and at most
$21.7$\,ms including the planner, i.e., about $1.8$\,ms per UE, far below the
$\Delta=1$\,s epoch.

\section{Simulation Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Training}
All variants use the environment and parameters of Table~\ref{tab:params}.
Each variant is trained for $I=2000$ iterations with five independent
training seeds $\{7,8,9,10,11\}$; the seed determines both the network
initialization and the training-environment realization. The five M1--M5
variants therefore comprise $25$ independently trained policies. Training
episodes contain the randomized disturbance schedule of
Section~\ref{sec:system}; the compound-stress pattern is never used for
training. To isolate goal regulation, we also train M1D, the memoryless M1
trained with $r_\mu$ and the dual controller of M5, with the same five seeds;
the multiplier enters its critic input but not its actor input, although the
actor is trained on $r_\mu$.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
Every trained policy is evaluated on five held-out evaluation seeds with $40$
episodes each under the \emph{nominal} and \emph{compound-stress} conditions
(Section~\ref{sec:system}). All methods are evaluated on identical,
mutually disjoint episode streams, which yields paired comparisons. Actions
are sampled from the stochastic policies, prices are reset at the start of
each episode, and the multiplier of M5 is frozen. For a learned variant, each
training seed is first averaged over the evaluation seeds, and the mean and
the standard deviation are computed across the five training seeds;
differences between learned variants are tested with Welch's $t$-test on the
per-seed means, and ``significant'' refers to the $5\%$ level. Rule-based
schemes are reported as means over the evaluation seeds. Besides the realized
outage ratio, we report the service deficit, the handover (HO) executions per epoch
(summed over the $N$ UEs), the ping-pong ratio (ping-pongs per successful
handover), the resource cost $C_{\mathrm{ctrl}}$, and the \emph{common reward}
$r$ of~\eqref{eq:reward}, which is used for all methods including M5.

\subsection{Baselines}
All baselines observe the same delayed and noisy telemetry and are subject
to the same action masks and admission rules as the learned agents.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{CHO (A3/TTT)}: a 3GPP-inspired CHO that prepares the best
observed candidate when it exceeds the serving satellite by $1$\,dB, executes
it when it exceeds the serving satellite by an execution offset for a
time-to-trigger (TTT), and releases it after it falls $1$\,dB below the
serving satellite for three epochs. The offset ($2$, $3$, $4$\,dB) and the TTT
($1$, $2$, $3$ epochs) were tuned for the common reward on three validation
seeds disjoint from all evaluation episodes and frozen at $2$\,dB and one
epoch, the most responsive values of the grid.
\item \emph{CHO (A3/TTT)+Dup}: the tuned CHO rule, additionally activating
duplication while a target is prepared and the serving SINR is below a
threshold tuned in the same way ($6$\,dB, selected from $\{2,4,6\}$\,dB).
\item \emph{Max-SINR}: prepares the best observed candidate when it exceeds
the serving satellite by $1$\,dB and executes as soon as allowed, without
duplication.
\item \emph{Always-Dup}: always prepares the best observed candidate,
activates duplication, and executes.
\item \emph{Threshold-Dup}: prepares the best candidate when the serving SINR
falls below $4$\,dB, activates duplication, and executes when the target
exceeds the serving satellite by $1$\,dB.
\end{itemize}
Learning-based handover schemes from the literature
\cite{yang2023dqn,liu2024marlho,yang2024fingerprints,zhao2026hrl} select a
serving satellite or trigger a handover, but they do not expose preparation,
duplication, release, and rollback actions and therefore cannot be applied
to the considered action space without redesign. We therefore use two
learned baselines that operate on the same observation space, action space,
action mask, and team reward $r$ as the proposed agents and are trained with
the same budget and seeds. \emph{MAPPO (M1)} is the policy-based CTDE baseline using only the local
perception input, i.e., without recurrent memory, ephemeris lookahead,
congestion prices, or dual regulation~\cite{yu2022mappo}. \emph{MADQN} (multi-agent deep Q-network) is a value-based
baseline following the fingerprint approach of \cite{yang2024fingerprints}:
all UEs share one double deep Q-network ($[128,128]$) whose input includes the
training progress and exploration rate, trained with experience replay, a
target network, and $\epsilon$-greedy exploration ($1\to0.05$ over the first
$40\%$ of the iterations). Its learning rate was selected from
$\{10^{-4},3\cdot10^{-4},10^{-3}\}$ on the CHO validation seeds ($10^{-3}$),
and it is evaluated with greedy actions, as is standard for value-based
methods; to exclude an effect of this choice, M1, M1D, and M5 are additionally
evaluated with argmax actions.

\subsection{Comparison with Baselines}
Table~\ref{tab:main} and Fig.~\ref{fig:comparison} compare M5 with all five
rule-based schemes and with the two learned baselines. All learned methods achieve lower outage ratios than the rule-based schemes:
in the nominal condition, their outage ratios are $0.119$--$0.149$, whereas the rules attain
$0.322$--$0.429$. Relative to the five rules, M5
lowers the outage ratio by $58$--$68\%$ and the service deficit by
$49$--$64\%$, raises the packet delivery ratio from $80.3$--$85.6\%$ to
$92.6\%$, and reduces the deadline-violation rate by $49$--$70\%$ ($2.2\%$ versus $4.3$--$7.5\%$); under compound stress, the outage reduction is
$44$--$56\%$ ($46\%$ relative to CHO). M5 also has a $79$--$91\%$ lower
ping-pong ratio and $67$--$79\%$ fewer failed handovers than CHO, CHO+Dup,
Max-SINR, and Always-Dup. Threshold-Dup has lower mobility counts only
because it rarely hands over, which yields the highest outage ratio.

Among the learned methods, the value-based MADQN attains the lowest mean
outage ratio ($0.119$), a significantly lower service deficit than M5, and
significantly fewer failed handovers than M1 and M5, whereas its outage difference to M1 and M5 is not significant; under compound
stress, no outage difference among MADQN, M1, and M5 is significant, whereas
M5 retains a slightly higher service deficit than M1 in both conditions
(significant). M5, in contrast, has the lowest ping-pong ratio of all learned methods ($0.035$
versus $0.069$ for MADQN and $0.118$ for M1), a significantly lower preparation
rejection rate than MADQN ($20\%$ versus $44\%$), and a stable resource use,
whereas two MADQN policies duplicate in $2.6\%$ and $5.2\%$ of the time. Across training seeds, the standard deviations of M5 are
also $3.2$, $1.7$, and $5.2$ times smaller than those of MADQN for the outage
ratio, the handover executions, and the ping-pong ratio. These findings
do not stem from the action-selection rule: with argmax actions, the outage
ratios of M1, M1D, and M5 change by at most $0.5$ percentage points (pp), whereas their handover
executions and resource costs decrease by $10$--$21\%$ and $36$--$41\%$, so
that M5 attains the fewest handover executions ($0.196$), the lowest ping-pong
ratio ($0.031$), and the lowest resource cost of all learned methods, while
the outage advantage of MADQN persists and argmax M1 and MADQN are
statistically indistinguishable in all metrics. A well-tuned value-based learner therefore provides a strong reliability
baseline; M5 does not improve the outage ratio over MADQN or M1 but yields a
lower ping-pong ratio and smaller across-seed variability.

The reliability gap relative to CHO also holds at the policy--scenario level:
in each condition, all $125$ policy--scenario pairs ($25$ trained policies
$\times$ $5$ evaluation seeds) have a lower outage ratio than CHO in the same
scenario ($250$ comparisons in total), and the
ratio between the outage of M5 and that of CHO stays within $0.38$--$0.42$
(nominal) and $0.52$--$0.56$ (stress) across the evaluation seeds. The learned
agents also carry more traffic than CHO (utilization of about $50\%$ versus
$36\%$) at a lower peak requested pressure ($1.13$--$1.20$ versus $1.35$).
This reliability improvement uses more control resources than CHO, which
almost never duplicates and thus spends $2.6\times10^{-3}$ versus $4.3\times10^{-3}$
for M5. A fixed SINR duplication trigger barely changes CHO (duplication active in
$0.06\%$ of the time). Under compound stress, whose burst and
blockage severities lie outside the training range, all learned methods remain
far more reliable than the rules, but their outage ratio increases by
$5.9$--$7.0$\,pp, compared with $2.5$\,pp for CHO and $3.0$\,pp for Max-SINR,
i.e., they are more sensitive to out-of-distribution disturbances.

\subsection{Capability-Structured Comparison}
\label{sec:ladder}
Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder} and Table~\ref{tab:main} show that the capability
ladder is not monotone and that the capabilities mainly change \emph{how}
reliability is achieved. The memoryless M1 attains the lowest outage ratio
among M1--M5 and the best common reward, but with the most mobility actions.
\emph{Memory} (M2) halves the ping-pong ratio and reduces handover executions
by $21\%$ at a $1.3$\,pp higher outage ratio, which is consistent with fewer reactions to transient telemetry
fluctuations. \emph{Reasoning} (M3) yields no measurable gain
over M2, and \emph{coordination} alone (M4) gives mixed results. The largest
change occurs when \emph{goal regulation} is added (M4$\to$M5): handover
executions decrease by $35\%$ and the ping-pong ratio by $53\%$ (both
significant), and the standard deviation across trained policies shrinks from
$0.092$ to $0.037$ for the handover executions and from $0.031$ to $0.006$
for the ping-pong ratio. Compared with M1, M5 performs $39\%$ fewer handover
executions and has a $70\%$ lower ping-pong ratio (both significant) at a non-significant $0.6$\,pp outage difference and a
significant $0.5$\,pp higher service deficit; under compound stress, the
reductions are $34\%$ and $66\%$. M1 and M5 therefore form two distinct
operating points.

To isolate goal regulation, M1D applies the Lagrangian reward to the
memoryless policy. Compared with M1, M1D lowers the ping-pong ratio by $38\%$
(significant) and the handover executions by $28\%$ (not significant) at a
statistically indistinguishable outage ratio ($0.126$) and common reward, and
all $50$ M1D policy--scenario pairs ($25$ per condition) again outperform CHO. Goal regulation therefore produces the largest isolated reduction in handover
executions among the tested additions, whereas memory alone yields the larger
ping-pong reduction ($49\%$ versus $38\%$). The remaining capabilities
still change the operating point: relative to M1D, M5 halves the ping-pong
ratio again ($-51\%$, significant) with markedly smaller standard deviations
across trained policies, but has a significantly higher outage ratio
($+1.0$\,pp), a lower common reward, and $24\%$ more preparation requests.
Post hoc, M5 overtakes M1 in common reward only when handover executions are
weighted about $30$--$35$ times more strongly than in training, whereas M1D
exceeds M5 for all tested weights. Memory, reasoning, and coordination thus trade a small
amount of reliability for further ping-pong suppression and training
consistency.

\subsection{Reliance on Coordination and Reasoning Signals}
To test whether the trained policies rely on the price and on the planner, we
re-evaluate them on the same episodes with the price forced to zero (M4, M5)
or with an uninformative reasoning feature (M3--M5), i.e., $\phi_{i,a}=0$ for
feasible and $\phi_{i,a}=1$ for masked actions, the value observed during
training whenever all plan costs are tied (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}; changes
in \%). A policy is counted as degraded
when its handover executions, ping-pong ratio, resource cost, or outage ratio
increase, or when its common reward decreases. Removing the price increases
the handover executions by $18$--$24\%$, the ping-pong ratio by $22$--$35\%$,
and the resource cost by $20$--$26\%$ in all ten M4/M5 policies and in both
conditions; the outage ratio increases in all ten policies in the nominal
condition ($+5$--$7\%$) and in eight of ten under stress, where the change is
small and not significant. M3 shows little sensitivity to the removal of the reasoning feature: its
changes remain within about $3\%$ without a consistent direction. In M4 and
M5, however, the reasoning feature also contains the price-aware plan cost of
\eqref{eq:plancost}; removing it increases the handover executions by
$6$--$8\%$, the ping-pong ratio by $8$--$10\%$, and the resource use by
$3$--$6\%$ across all ten policies and both conditions. The reasoning input
therefore affects the trained policies primarily when it is coupled with
congestion information. Because removing a trained input creates a
distribution shift, these ablations measure within-policy reliance rather than
the causal benefit of training with the signal.

\subsection{Conditional-Handover Behavior}
Fig.~\ref{fig:behavior} shows that the learned agents use the CHO procedure
differently from the rules. Once the tuned CHO rule prepares a target, it
executes the switch in $73\%$ of the cases after holding the preparation for
$5.3$ epochs, and $49\%$ of its requests are rejected because many UEs request
the same satellite at the same time. The learned agents instead treat a
preparation as a short-lived \emph{option}. M5 issues a preparation in $38\%$
of its decisions and releases most of them, so that only $6.3\%$ of the
preparations end in a switch, and it holds a preparation for only $1.3$
epochs. Across M1--M5, the prepare-to-commit ratio decreases from $18.9\%$ for M1
to $9.7$--$13.5\%$ for M2--M4 and $6.3\%$ for M5, while the preparation
rejection rate decreases from $35\%$ to $20\%$. PDCP duplication is used sparingly by the MAPPO-based variants (active
$0.2$--$0.6\%$ of the time), whereas activating it indiscriminately
(Always-Dup) degrades every metric. This behavior increases control-plane signaling: M5
issues $4.6$ preparation requests per epoch, compared with $1.0$ for CHO,
which the present reward does not penalize (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\emph{Reliability target.} The outage ratios of M5 and M1D on the held-out
scenarios ($0.137$ and $0.126$) remain above the threshold
$C_{\mathrm{thr}}=0.10$, as do those of all other variants, and changing the
frozen multiplier at evaluation hardly changes the behavior of M5. The dual
controller should therefore be understood as a training-time regulator rather
than as a goal-conditioned policy; enforcing the constraint on unseen
scenarios and training with randomized multipliers are open problems.

\emph{Reasoning.} M3 shows little behavioral dependence on the purely
geometric planner, whereas the price-aware planner of M4 and M5 affects the
trained policies. The planner extrapolates delayed
telemetry, treats a feasible switch as deterministic, and sees control-slot
contention only through the price; an expected handover-success model, an explicit contention term, and
planner-based action priors are natural next steps. Why the Lagrangian reward
alone suppresses unnecessary handovers (M1D), and why M5 further reduces
ping-pong and training variability, also remain open questions.

\emph{Signaling and duplication.} The reward charges reserved preparation
resources but not preparation messages, so the learned agents issue
$3.6$--$4.9$ times more preparation requests than CHO; a message-level
signaling cost is needed to confirm that the advantages persist when
signaling is priced. The regimes in which duplication becomes worthwhile
(e.g., stricter reliability classes or lower duplication cost) also remain to
be characterized.

\emph{Model scope.} The study uses a stylized slice with six satellites and
twelve UEs, a fixed interference floor, a system-level protocol abstraction,
a fixed scheduler, and five trained policies per variant. Larger
constellations, explicit interference, and message-level protocol effects
remain to be evaluated.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We formulated the joint control of CHO preparation, PDCP duplication, and
primary-satellite switching in a shared-capacity LEO network as a constrained
Dec-POMDP and developed a capability-structured multi-agent framework with
memory, ephemeris-based lookahead, congestion-price coordination, and
training-time reliability regulation. Every learned policy--scenario pair
achieved a lower outage ratio than the tuned 3GPP-inspired CHO rule, and M5
reduced the outage ratio by $60\%$ under nominal conditions and by $46\%$
under compound stress relative to CHO. Compared with the memoryless MAPPO
policy, M5 attained a statistically indistinguishable outage ratio with $39\%$
fewer handover executions and a $70\%$ lower ping-pong ratio. A tuned
multi-agent DQN provided a strong reliability baseline, while M5 produced a
lower ping-pong ratio and smaller across-seed variability in mobility behavior.
Ablations showed that goal regulation produced the largest isolated reduction in handover executions, that the M4 and M5 policies relied on congestion prices, and that the
reasoning feature affected behavior mainly when its plan cost included the
price term. The learned policies used CHO preparations primarily as short-lived
reservations. Given the limitations of Section~\ref{sec:limitations}, future
work should evaluate goal-conditioned regulation, planner-guided action
selection, and signaling-aware rewards.
