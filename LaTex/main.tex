\title{Capability-Structured Agentic Multi-Agent Control of Conditional Handover and PDCP Duplication in LEO Satellite Networks}
\author{\todo{Authors and affiliations}}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Low Earth orbit (LEO) satellite networks force user equipments (UEs) to
change serving satellites frequently, while conditional handover (CHO)
and packet data convergence protocol (PDCP) duplication expose new but
coupled control options: when to prepare a candidate, when to duplicate,
and when to switch. We formulate the joint control of CHO preparation,
PDCP duplication, and primary-satellite switching among UEs that share
finite satellite and preparation resources as a constrained decentralized
partially observable Markov decision process with delayed, noisy
telemetry and hidden link degradation. We then propose a
capability-structured agentic framework built on centralized training
with decentralized execution, in which perception, recurrent memory,
ephemeris-based reasoning, congestion-price coordination, and a
primal--dual reliability regulator are introduced as separable
capabilities without placing a language model in the control loop.
Across $25$ independently trained policies, every learned
policy--scenario pair achieves a lower outage ratio than a tuned
3GPP-inspired A3/TTT CHO rule. For the full M5 architecture, the mean
outage reduction relative to CHO is $60\%$ under nominal conditions and
$46\%$ under compound stress. Compared with the memoryless M1 policy, M5
achieves nearly the same reliability with $39\%$ fewer handover
executions and a $70\%$ lower ping-pong ratio. Within-policy ablations
further show that all ten price-enabled M4/M5 policies exploit the
congestion signal to suppress unnecessary mobility and redundant
resource usage. A behavioral analysis reveals that
the agents use CHO preparations as short-lived options and commit only
$6\%$ of them. We also report the limitations of the approach, including
increased preparation signalling and higher sensitivity to compound
disturbances.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Agentic AI, conditional handover, PDCP duplication, LEO satellite
networks, multi-agent reinforcement learning, non-terrestrial networks.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
% =====================================================================

Non-terrestrial networks (NTNs) are expected to play a key role in
sixth-generation (6G) wireless systems by extending broadband
connectivity beyond the coverage of terrestrial infrastructure
\cite{giordani2021ntn,3gpp38811,fontanesi2026aisurvey}.
Among different NTN platforms, low Earth orbit (LEO) satellite
constellations are particularly attractive because their relatively low
altitude provides lower propagation delay and path loss than
geostationary satellite systems while enabling large-scale geographical
coverage \cite{giordani2021ntn,juan2022handover}.
The rapid orbital motion that enables such large-scale coverage,
however, also makes mobility management a fundamental challenge.
Satellite visibility continuously changes, serving opportunities are
time limited, and user equipments (UEs) may experience frequent
inter-satellite handovers even when they remain geographically
stationary \cite{ji2021mobility,juan2022handover}.

Existing LEO mobility studies have addressed this problem from several
directions. Classical schemes select serving satellites according to
remaining visibility time, elevation angle, link quality, or satellite
load, and graph- or flow-based formulations coordinate multiple users
under constellation-wide constraints
\cite{zhang2021networkflow,juan2022handover}.
Location-aware conditional handover (CHO) further exploits predictable
satellite motion to improve handover triggering
\cite{lee2023locationcho}, whereas AcceHo reduces handover latency by
leveraging deterministic satellite trajectories and reducing
access--core network interaction \cite{wu2024acceho}.
At a different layer, SKYCASTLE redesigns mobility management around
distributed satellite anchors to mitigate service interruptions caused
by the joint mobility of users and satellite infrastructure
\cite{li2024skycastle}.
These studies demonstrate that satellite mobility is sufficiently
structured to support proactive control rather than purely reactive
signal-strength-based handover.

Multi-connectivity (MC) provides an additional mechanism for improving
service continuity. By allowing a UE to simultaneously exploit multiple
radio paths, MC can increase throughput, provide path redundancy, and
improve reliability \cite{pupiales2021mc,lopez2022empiricalmc}.
3GPP has investigated multi-connectivity and service continuity for NTN
architectures \cite{3gpp38821}, while NR Radio Resource Control (RRC)
supports conditional reconfiguration and CHO
\cite{3gpp38331}. At the user plane, Packet Data Convergence Protocol
(PDCP) supports packet duplication over multiple lower-layer entities,
including explicit activation and deactivation of duplication
\cite{3gpp38323}. These mechanisms are complementary: a candidate link
can be prepared before a mobility event, duplicated transmission can
temporarily protect service continuity, and the primary serving link can
subsequently be switched when appropriate.

Nevertheless, exposing these additional protocol options also creates a
more difficult control problem. Aggressive candidate preparation can
consume limited preparation resources, persistent packet duplication
increases radio-resource usage, and frequent switching can cause
unnecessary handovers and ping-pong behavior. Conversely, delaying
preparation or duplication may expose the UE to service degradation or
outage when the serving satellite becomes unavailable. Furthermore,
the decisions of different UEs are inherently coupled because they
compete for shared satellite capacity and candidate-preparation
opportunities. Consequently, locally attractive mobility decisions may
be collectively inefficient when many UEs simultaneously target the
same satellite.

Recent learning-based approaches have demonstrated the ability of
reinforcement learning (RL) to address the dynamic and multi-attribute
nature of satellite handover. DQN-based methods adapt handover
decisions to changing network conditions
\cite{yang2023dqn}, while multi-agent deep RL has been developed to
account for dynamic propagation conditions, satellite capacity, and
distributed user decisions \cite{liu2024marlho,yang2024fingerprints}.
GEMS further incorporates spatial and temporal satellite mobility into
dual-connectivity configuration using graph attention
\cite{wang2026gems}. These approaches substantially improve the
adaptability of satellite mobility management. However, most existing
learning-based schemes focus primarily on \emph{which} satellite to
select or \emph{when} a handover should occur. The sequential and
stateful interaction among candidate preparation, temporary
duplication, primary-link switching, release, and rollback remains
largely unexplored, particularly when observations are delayed or noisy
and multiple UEs share finite satellite resources.

These characteristics motivate a control architecture that goes beyond
one-shot handover selection. A mobility controller must retain temporal
context to distinguish transient and persistent degradation, reason
about the future availability of candidate satellites, coordinate with
other UEs under shared resource constraints, and adapt its actions
toward reliability objectives. Such requirements are closely related to
the emerging notion of Agentic AI. Recent studies characterize Agentic
AI through autonomous perception, contextual reasoning, persistent
memory, goal-directed action, and multi-agent coordination
\cite{sapkota2026agentic,jiang2026tutorial,zhang2026edgeagentic}.
Advanced DRL has also been identified as an important mechanism for
realizing sequential and adaptive agentic control in wireless networks
\cite{zheng2026agenticdrl}.

Agentic AI in communication networks does not necessarily imply that a
large language model (LLM) must directly execute every real-time control
action. LLM-centric approaches have recently been investigated for
satellite spectrum orchestration, where natural-language service
requirements and spectrum observations are processed to generate
resource-allocation strategies \cite{ni2026llmleo}. In contrast, AERS
demonstrates a decentralized Agentic-AI architecture in which
autonomous beam agents collaboratively perform satellite resource
scheduling through learning and environmental interaction
\cite{shen2026aers}. Agentic multi-agent learning has also been applied
to task-oriented resource orchestration in satellite-assisted vehicular
networks \cite{xie2026agenticiov}. These studies suggest that the
appropriate realization of agentic intelligence depends on the
timescale and structure of the underlying network-control problem.

Motivated by these observations, we propose a
\emph{capability-structured agentic multi-agent mobility framework} for
joint CHO and PDCP-duplication control in shared-capacity LEO satellite
networks. Each UE acts as an autonomous agent that controls candidate
preparation, duplication activation/deactivation, primary-link
switching, release, and rollback. The framework employs centralized
training with decentralized execution (CTDE) based on multi-agent
proximal policy optimization (MAPPO), while explicitly separating the
capabilities required for robust mobility control. Recurrent memory
summarizes temporally correlated observations, a protocol-aware
reasoning module exploits deterministic ephemeris information to assess
future candidate viability, and a lightweight satellite congestion
price communicates shared-resource pressure without exchanging the
full states or actions of other UEs. A reliability-oriented dual
controller further regulates the outage objective during training.

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows.

\begin{itemize}
    \item We formulate joint CHO preparation, PDCP-duplication control,
    and primary-satellite switching as a decentralized partially
    observable sequential decision problem in a shared-capacity LEO
    network. The formulation captures delayed and noisy observations,
    time-varying satellite visibility, hidden link degradation,
    preparation contention, duplication overhead, and mobility cost.

    \item We develop a capability-structured CTDE-MAPPO framework in
    which perception, recurrent memory, ephemeris-assisted reasoning,
    and resource-aware coordination are introduced as distinct mobility
    capabilities. This design supports capability-structured comparisons
    and targeted within-policy ablations without relying on a monolithic
    LLM-based controller.

    \item We introduce a lightweight coordination mechanism based on
    satellite congestion prices. The price summarizes shared-resource
    pressure and influences decentralized mobility decisions without
    requiring UE-to-UE exchange of local observations or actions. A
    primal--dual reliability controller is further incorporated to
    regulate outage-related constraints during training.

    \item We conduct protocol- and capability-level evaluations using
    conventional handover and duplication heuristics, a
    3GPP-inspired A3/TTT CHO baseline, independently trained capability
    variants, targeted reasoning and coordination ablations, and
    compound traffic, capacity, and blockage disturbances. The
    evaluation characterizes not only service reliability but also
    duplication overhead, handover activity, ping-pong behavior, and
    shared-resource usage.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{LEO Mobility Management and Conditional Handover}

The high orbital velocity of LEO satellites makes mobility management
fundamentally different from that in terrestrial cellular networks.
Satellite and user mobility jointly produce time-varying coverage,
frequent handovers, and additional signaling and decision-making
overhead \cite{ji2021mobility}. Conventional LEO handover schemes
therefore consider satellite-specific information such as elevation
angle, remaining visibility time, channel availability, and load.
For example, Zhang \emph{et al.} formulate multi-user satellite
handover as a network-flow problem whose edge weights reflect user
requests and satellite service quality
\cite{zhang2021networkflow}. Juan \emph{et al.} systematically examine
5G NR handover procedures in LEO networks and exploit predictable
satellite motion and antenna information for mobility control
\cite{juan2022handover}.

The predictability of satellite motion has also motivated proactive
handover mechanisms. Lee \emph{et al.} propose location-based CHO for
LEO NTN, using the relative distance between a UE and serving/target
cell reference locations rather than relying only on received signal
strength \cite{lee2023locationcho}. AcceHo reduces handover latency by
predicting future satellite access and avoiding repeated interaction
with the core network during the handover procedure
\cite{wu2024acceho}. At the network layer, SKYCASTLE deploys
distributed satellite anchors and convergence-free forwarding to
mitigate connection interruption and latency caused by the mobility of
both users and LEO infrastructure \cite{li2024skycastle}. These works
establish the importance of proactive satellite mobility management,
but they do not jointly determine candidate preparation, temporary
PDCP duplication, and subsequent primary-link switching.

\subsection{Multi-Connectivity and PDCP Duplication}

Multi-connectivity enables a UE to simultaneously exploit radio
resources from multiple access points and can improve throughput,
service continuity, and reliability through traffic aggregation or
path redundancy \cite{pupiales2021mc}. Its potential in integrated
terrestrial--satellite networks has also been demonstrated
experimentally. Lopez \emph{et al.} evaluate simultaneous terrestrial
5G and Starlink connectivity and show the potential of MC for reducing
high-end latency and improving continuous service availability
\cite{lopez2022empiricalmc}.

3GPP NTN studies explicitly consider multi-connectivity involving
NTN-based NG-RAN and service continuity between NTN and terrestrial
networks \cite{3gpp38821}. At the RRC layer, conditional
reconfiguration enables candidate mobility configurations to be stored
and conditionally executed \cite{3gpp38331}. At the user plane, the
NR PDCP specification defines routing and duplication functions and
supports activation/deactivation of PDCP duplication
\cite{3gpp38323}. These standardized mechanisms provide the protocol
building blocks for reliable mobility, but the standards intentionally
leave many control decisions to network implementation.

Recent work has therefore studied how secondary links should be
selected and maintained. GEMS formulates TN--NTN and NTN--NTN dual
connectivity as a spatial matching problem and extends the graph
temporally to account for future satellite mobility
\cite{wang2026gems}. Such work demonstrates that continuity-aware MC
must account for both current link utility and future service windows.
Our problem is complementary but different: rather than determining
only the dual-connectivity association, we control the
\emph{lifecycle} of the mobility procedure---preparation, duplication,
commit, release, and rollback---under shared preparation and radio
capacity.

\subsection{Learning-Based Satellite Mobility Control}

The dynamics and large state spaces of satellite networks have
motivated the use of RL for adaptive mobility management. Yang
\emph{et al.} develop the DQN-ALrM handover method for
satellite--ground integrated networks, using a customized DQN with an
adaptive learning rate to handle long-term multi-metric handover
optimization \cite{yang2023dqn}. Liu \emph{et al.} consider
mega-constellations under dynamic propagation conditions and develop
centralized and distributed multi-agent deep-RL handover schemes that
account for user-rate requirements and satellite load
\cite{liu2024marlho}.

Distributed multi-agent control has also been investigated to reduce
centralized signaling and computational overhead. Yang \emph{et al.}
formulate LEO handover as a decentralized decision process and employ
multi-agent fingerprints with DDQN to mitigate training
non-stationarity and load imbalance
\cite{yang2024fingerprints}. GEMS uses graph attention to incorporate
future satellite mobility into connectivity decisions
\cite{wang2026gems}. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that
learning-based policies can outperform static or myopic handover
rules in dynamic environments.

However, existing learning-based mobility solutions predominantly
treat handover as a serving-target or trigger decision. A CHO and
PDCP-duplication procedure instead has an internal protocol state:
a target may first be prepared, duplication may subsequently be
activated or deactivated, a switch may be committed, or the candidate
may be released or rolled back. Actions at one control epoch therefore
constrain the feasible actions and resource consumption at subsequent
epochs. Moreover, multiple UE policies interact through finite
satellite capacity and preparation resources. Our framework explicitly
models this stateful protocol evolution and multi-user coupling under
partial observability.

\subsection{Agentic AI for Wireless and Satellite Networks}

Agentic AI has recently emerged as a broader paradigm for autonomous
network intelligence. Recent taxonomies associate Agentic AI with
capabilities including contextual perception, persistent memory,
reasoning, goal-directed behavior, autonomous action, and multi-agent
coordination \cite{sapkota2026agentic,zhang2026edgeagentic}.
The recent JSAC tutorial on future intelligent communications further
describes Agentic AI as a closed-loop architecture that integrates
perception, reasoning, planning, tools, and memory for continuous
interaction with dynamic communication environments
\cite{jiang2026tutorial}. Advanced DRL is consequently receiving
attention as an enabling technology for autonomous and adaptive
wireless agents that must make sequential decisions
\cite{zheng2026agenticdrl}.

Current communication-oriented Agentic-AI research follows several
implementation paths. LLM-centric approaches exploit semantic
reasoning and natural-language interaction. For example, Ni
\emph{et al.} employ an LLM as a spectrum-orchestration engine that
combines spectrum observations with natural-language QoS descriptions
to produce LEO resource-allocation strategies
\cite{ni2026llmleo}, and the Think--Do architecture separates
high-level LLM reasoning from real-time execution for satellite
resource orchestration \cite{cao2026thinkdo}. Such approaches are
particularly useful when a controller must interpret human intent or
unstructured knowledge.

A complementary direction focuses on autonomous domain-specific agents
whose perception and action spaces are directly tied to network
control. AERS models individual satellite beams as autonomous agents
that coordinate beam hopping, user scheduling, and power allocation
under shared satellite constraints \cite{shen2026aers}. Xie
\emph{et al.} integrate multi-agent learning and task-oriented
attention for distributed resource orchestration in
satellite-assisted IoV \cite{xie2026agenticiov}. More broadly, recent
surveys identify AI/ML as an important enabler for adaptive satellite
communication while emphasizing the computational constraints of
on-board implementation \cite{fontanesi2026aisurvey}.

Our work adopts this capability-oriented interpretation of Agentic AI.
It does not place an LLM in the real-time mobility-control loop.
Instead, domain-specific mechanisms instantiate the capabilities
required by the considered problem: recurrent state provides temporal
memory, deterministic ephemeris acts as a physics-grounded reasoning
tool, congestion prices provide lightweight multi-agent coordination,
and a dual controller supplies goal-oriented reliability regulation.
The resulting architecture targets a protocol-control timescale at
which predictable orbital dynamics and structured radio states can be
exploited directly, without requiring unrestricted semantic reasoning
or an LLM in the real-time control loop.

\section{System Model}
\label{sec:system}
% =====================================================================

We consider the downlink of a non-terrestrial network in which a set
$\Lset=\{1,\dots,L\}$ of low Earth orbit (LEO) satellites serves a set
$\Nset=\{1,\dots,N\}$ of ground user equipments (UEs). Time is slotted into
control epochs $t\in\{0,1,\dots,T-1\}$ of duration $\Delta$; mobility
decisions are taken once per epoch. Each UE is an autonomous decision-making
agent that controls its own conditional handover (CHO) and packet data
convergence protocol (PDCP) duplication procedure, while all UEs share the
finite radio and control-plane resources of the satellites. Table~\ref{tab:params}
summarizes the parameters.

\emph{Modeling scope and protocol abstraction.}
Our objective is to study the decision coupling among target preparation,
PDCP duplication, and primary-cell switching rather than to reproduce the
complete signalling stack. Accordingly, the CHO procedure is represented by
a system-level state machine that abstracts the preparation, execution,
completion, and cooldown phases of conditional mobility, while PDCP
duplication is represented by the synchronized transmission of the same
packet stream over the serving and the prepared target legs. This
abstraction is inspired by the NR conditional-reconfiguration procedure of
3GPP TS~38.331 and the PDCP functionality of
3GPP TS~38.323~\cite{3gpp38331,3gpp38323}. It does not model individual RRC
messages, RLC entities, HARQ processes, or radio-link-failure recovery. The
radio scheduler is kept fixed throughout all experiments, so that the
learned agents control only mobility preparation, duplication, and switching.

\subsection{Constellation Geometry and Visibility}

Satellite $\ell$ moves on a circular orbit of altitude $h$ and inclination
$\iota$. Its Earth-centred position is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}_\ell(t) = (R_E\!+\!h)\big[\cos\theta_\ell,\,\sin\theta_\ell\cos\iota,\,\sin\theta_\ell\sin\iota\big]^{\mathsf T}\!,
\label{eq:orbit}
\end{equation}
with orbital phase $\theta_\ell(t)=\theta_\ell^{0}+s_\ell\,\omega\,t\Delta$,
angular rate $\omega=2\pi/T_{\mathrm{orb}}$, initial phases $\theta_\ell^0$
evenly spaced over $[-0.38,0.38]$\,rad, and direction $s_\ell=+1$ for
$\theta_\ell^0\le0$ and $s_\ell=-1$ otherwise. This construction represents a
stylized local constellation slice with overlapping satellite passes rather
than a full Walker constellation. It creates repeated rise--set transitions
and simultaneous handover opportunities while keeping the mobility-control
experiment computationally tractable. UE $i$ is fixed at
$\mathbf{u}_i$ on the Earth surface, with latitude and longitude drawn
uniformly within a $\pm0.04$\,rad $\times$ $\pm0.05$\,rad area. The
elevation angle $\epsilon_{i\ell}(t)$ and slant range $d_{i\ell}(t)$ follow
from $\mathbf{p}_\ell(t)-\mathbf{u}_i$, and satellite $\ell$ is visible to UE
$i$ if $v_{i\ell}(t)=\ind\{\epsilon_{i\ell}(t)\ge\epsilon_{\min}\}=1$.

\subsection{Channel and Link Abstraction}

The received power of the link $(i,\ell)$ in dBm is
\begin{multline}
P^{\mathrm{rx}}_{i\ell}(t) = P^{\mathrm{tx}} + G^{\mathrm{tx}} + G^{\mathrm{rx}}
 - \mathrm{FSPL}\big(d_{i\ell}(t)\big) - L_{\mathrm{atm}} - L_{\mathrm{impl}} \\
 - L_{\mathrm{el}}\big(\epsilon_{i\ell}(t)\big) - \chi_{i\ell}(t) - B_{i\ell}(t) - Z_{i\ell}(t),
\label{eq:rx}
\end{multline}
where $\mathrm{FSPL}(d)=20\log_{10}(4\pi d f_c/c)$,
$L_{\mathrm{el}}(\epsilon)=1.5/\max\{0.2,\sin\epsilon\}$\,dB models the
additional low-elevation loss, and $\chi_{i\ell}(t)$ is log-normal shadowing
with first-order autoregressive (AR(1)) temporal correlation and $w_{i\ell}(t)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$,
\begin{equation}
\chi_{i\ell}(t)=\rho_\chi\,\chi_{i\ell}(t-1)+\sigma_\chi\sqrt{1-\rho_\chi^2}\,w_{i\ell}(t),
\end{equation}
$B_{i\ell}(t)$ is the extra loss caused by blockage events and $Z_{i\ell}(t)$
is the loss of a hidden link-degradation regime (Section~\ref{sec:nonstat}).
Let $P_N=-174+10\log_{10}W+F_{\mathrm{dB}}$ denote the receiver noise power
in dBm, where $F_{\mathrm{dB}}$ is the receiver noise figure. Converting the
received signal, noise, and aggregate interference powers to mW, the
signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio (SINR) of a visible link is
\begin{equation}
\gamma_{i\ell}(t)=\frac{10^{P^{\mathrm{rx}}_{i\ell}(t)/10}}{10^{P_N/10}+10^{I_{\mathrm{dBm}}/10}},
\label{eq:sinr}
\end{equation}
and it is set to a negligibly small value otherwise. Here $I_{\mathrm{dBm}}$ is
a fixed aggregate interference floor; explicit co-channel interference
dynamics are therefore outside the scope of the present model. The link is
abstracted by a capped Shannon efficiency and a smooth block-error model,
\begin{align}
\eta_{i\ell}(t) &= \min\big\{\eta_{\max},\ 0.75\log_2\!\big(1+\gamma_{i\ell}(t)\big)\big\},\\
q_{i\ell}(t) &= \Big(1+\exp\!\big(-0.75\,(\gamma^{\mathrm{dB}}_{i\ell}(t)+2)\big)\Big)^{-1}\,v_{i\ell}(t),
\label{eq:bler}
\end{align}
where $q_{i\ell}$ is the per-packet delivery probability (50\% at $-2$\,dB).

\subsection{Traffic, Scheduling, and Service}

Packets of $S$ bits arrive at UE $i$ according to a Poisson process of rate
$\lambda_i\kappa_i(t)$, where $\lambda_i=\bar\lambda u_i$ with
$u_i\sim\mathcal{U}[1-\delta_\lambda,1+\delta_\lambda]$ captures traffic
heterogeneity and $\kappa_i(t)\ge1$ is a traffic-burst multiplier. Packets
wait in a finite queue $Q_i(t)\le Q_{\max}$ and are dropped when their age
exceeds the deadline $D$. A UE is active if $Q_i(t)>0$, and its required rate is
\begin{equation}
R_i(t)=\min\big\{\lambda_i\kappa_i(t),\ Q_i(t)S/\Delta\big\}.
\end{equation}

Each UE has a serving (primary) satellite $p_i(t)$ and, during a mobility
procedure, a target (secondary) satellite $c_i(t)$. A UE requests a data leg on
$p_i(t)$ and, when duplicating, a second leg on $c_i(t)$. The satellites run a
fixed QoS-aware weighted-fair scheduler that is \emph{not} controlled by the
agents. For a leg $(i,\ell)$ the requested resource fraction is
\begin{equation}
f_{i\ell}(t)=\min\Big\{1,\ \frac{R_i(t)}{W\,\eta_{i\ell}(t)\,a_\ell(t)}\Big\},
\end{equation}
where $a_\ell(t)=\max\{0.05,\,a^{0}\,m_\ell(t)\}$ is the effective capacity
scale and $m_\ell(t)\le1$ a capacity-drop multiplier. Let $n^{\mathrm{res}}_\ell(t)$
be the number of UEs holding a preparation reservation on $\ell$; each
reservation blocks a fraction $\rho_{\mathrm{prep}}$ of the satellite
resource, leaving $A_\ell(t)=1-\rho_{\mathrm{prep}}\,n^{\mathrm{res}}_\ell(t)$
for data. With weights $w_{i\ell}=f_{i\ell}\,\varpi_i$ ($\varpi_i$: QoS
priority), the granted share is $x_{i\ell}=w_{i\ell}$ if
$\sum_j w_{j\ell}\le A_\ell$ and $x_{i\ell}=A_\ell w_{i\ell}/\sum_j w_{j\ell}$
otherwise, which gives the leg rate
$r_{i\ell}(t)=x_{i\ell}(t)\,W\eta_{i\ell}(t)a_\ell(t)$. The requested
pressure of satellite $\ell$ is
$\Phi_\ell(t)=\rho_{\mathrm{prep}}n^{\mathrm{res}}_\ell(t)+\sum_i f_{i\ell}(t)$.
The scheduler is intentionally kept outside the learned action space.
Although the formulation allows heterogeneous priority weights $\varpi_i$,
all UEs use $\varpi_i=1$ in the reported experiments; the evaluated scheduler
is thus a demand-weighted fair allocator rather than a learned or
heterogeneous-QoS scheduler. This separation allows performance differences
among M1--M5 to be attributed to mobility preparation, duplication,
reasoning, and coordination decisions rather than to changes in
radio-resource scheduling.

\emph{Delivery.} Without duplication, UE $i$ transmits at
$r_i=r_{i p_i}$ with success probability $q_i=q_{ip_i}$. With PDCP
duplication, the same packets are sent on both legs, so that
$r_i=\min\{r_{ip_i},r_{ic_i}\}$ and
\begin{equation}
q_i^{\mathrm{dup}}=(1-\varkappa)\big(1-\bar q_{ip_i}\bar q_{ic_i}\big)+\varkappa\max\{q_{ip_i},q_{ic_i}\},
\label{eq:dup}
\end{equation}
where $\bar q=1-q$ and $\varkappa$ is a common-mode failure correlation. The number of
delivered packets is
$\mathrm{Bin}\big(\min\{\lfloor r_i\Delta/S\rfloor,Q_i\},\,q_i\big)$.
We define the expected goodput $g_i=\min\{r_i,Q_iS/\Delta\}\,q_i$ and the
realized goodput $\hat g_i$ (delivered bits per epoch).

\emph{Service metrics.} The normalized service deficit and the outage
indicator of an active UE are
\begin{equation}
\delta_i(t)=\frac{[R_i(t)-g_i(t)]^+}{R_i(t)},\qquad
o_i(t)=\ind\big\{\hat g_i(t)<\beta\,R_i(t)\big\},
\label{eq:service}
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ is the minimum acceptable service ratio. Note that $\delta_i$ is
a smooth expected-value quantity used for learning, whereas $o_i$ is the
realized, packet-level reliability event.

\subsection{Conditional Handover and PDCP Duplication Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

Each UE runs the five-state mobility state machine of
Fig.~\ref{fig:fsm}: \textsc{Single} (S), \textsc{Prepared} (P),
\textsc{Duplicating} (D), \textsc{Switching} (W), and \textsc{Cooldown} (C).
The set of candidate targets $\mathcal{C}_i(t)$ contains the $K$ visible
non-serving satellites with the highest \emph{observed} SINR
(Section~\ref{sec:obs}). The per-UE action set is
\begin{multline}
\Aset=\{\textsc{Stay},\,\textsc{Prep}_1,\dots,\textsc{Prep}_K,\,\textsc{DupOn},\\
\textsc{DupOff},\,\textsc{Commit},\,\textsc{Release},\,\textsc{Rollback}\},
\end{multline}
so $|\Aset|=K+6$. A state-dependent hard mask $\mathbf{m}_i(t)\in\{0,1\}^{|\Aset|}$
enforces protocol and physical validity:
\begin{itemize}
\item In S, $\textsc{Prep}_k$ requests CHO preparation towards the $k$-th
candidate. If admitted, the UE enters P with a preparation timer $T_{\mathrm{prep}}$.
\item In P (after the timer expires), $\textsc{DupOn}$ activates duplication (P$\to$D),
$\textsc{Commit}$ executes the CHO (P$\to$W), and $\textsc{Release}$ cancels
the preparation (P$\to$S).
\item In D, $\textsc{DupOff}$ returns to P, $\textsc{Commit}$ executes the
CHO, and $\textsc{Rollback}$ cancels both the duplication and the preparation.
\item $\textsc{Commit}$ is feasible only if the target is visible and the
dwell condition $t-t^{\mathrm{sw}}_i\ge T_{\mathrm{dwell}}$ holds, where
$t^{\mathrm{sw}}_i$ is the epoch of the last completed switch.
\end{itemize}
If the target becomes invisible while the UE is in P or D, the preparation is
lost and the UE returns to S. During the \textsc{Switching} state, the source
satellite remains the serving data leg until the switching outcome is resolved,
while the target preparation reservation is retained. Hence, entering W does not
immediately change the serving data path.

After $T_{\mathrm{sw}}$ epochs in W, the switch succeeds with probability
\begin{equation}
p^{\mathrm{HO}}_i(t)
=
p_0\,q_{ic_i}(t)\,
\exp\!\big(-[\Phi_{c_i}(t-1)-1]^+\big),
\label{eq:hosuccess}
\end{equation}
where $\Phi_{c_i}(t-1)$ is the most recently realized requested pressure
available when the switching outcome is resolved. The one-epoch offset follows
from the causal control sequence: the switching outcome is resolved before the
scheduler computes the requested pressure of the current epoch. The probability
therefore decreases with poor target-link quality and recently observed target
congestion.

On success, $c_i$ becomes the new serving satellite and the UE enters C for
$T_{\mathrm{cd}}$ epochs; otherwise it returns to S on the old serving
satellite. A successful switch back to the previous serving satellite within
$T_{\mathrm{pp}}$ epochs is counted as a ping-pong. Separate radio-link-failure
reattachment is not modeled; if the source data leg becomes unavailable before
the switching outcome is resolved, the corresponding service may experience an
outage.

\emph{Preparation admission.} CHO preparation consumes two separate
resources of the target satellite: a data-plane reservation
$\rho_{\mathrm{prep}}$ (above) and one of $M$ control-plane preparation slots.
All preparation requests of an epoch are collected first; if the number of new
requests towards satellite $\ell$ exceeds
$\min\{M-n^{\mathrm{res}}_\ell,\ \lfloor A_\ell/\rho_{\mathrm{prep}}\rfloor\}$,
the admitted requests are drawn uniformly at random and the others are
rejected. Admission is therefore an explicit multi-UE contention.

\subsection{Information Structure and Partial Observability}
\label{sec:obs}

The agents do not observe the complete physical state. Current geometric link
availability is assumed to be known for the serving-link visibility indicator
and candidate presence, whereas radio-quality, elevation, and load information
provided to the actor is delayed by $\tau_{\mathrm{obs}}$ epochs. In addition,
the observed SINR and load are corrupted by temporally correlated noise.
Specifically,
\begin{gather}
\tilde\gamma^{\mathrm{dB}}_{i\ell}(t)
=
\gamma^{\mathrm{dB}}_{i\ell}(t-\tau_{\mathrm{obs}})
+n_{i\ell}(t),
\label{eq:telemetry}\\
n_{i\ell}(t)
=
\rho_n n_{i\ell}(t-1)
+\sigma_\gamma\sqrt{1-\rho_n^2}\,w'_{i\ell}(t),
\notag\\
\tilde\eta_{i\ell}(t)
=
\min\!\Big\{
\eta_{\max},
\max\!\big\{
0,\,
\eta_{i\ell}(t-\tau_{\mathrm{obs}})\,
2^{n_{i\ell}(t)/10}
\big\}
\Big\},
\notag\\
\tilde\epsilon_{i\ell}(t)
=
\epsilon_{i\ell}(t-\tau_{\mathrm{obs}}),
\notag\\
\tilde\Phi_{i\ell}(t)
=
\big[
\Phi_\ell(t-1-\tau_{\mathrm{obs}})
+n^\Phi_{i\ell}(t)
\big]^+,
\notag
\end{gather}
where $n^\Phi_{i\ell}(t)$ follows the same AR(1) form with standard deviation
$\sigma_\Phi$. Thus, elevation telemetry is delayed but is not subjected to an
additional measurement-noise term in the present implementation.
The local observation $\mathbf{o}_i(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{11+5+7K}$ contains
(i) the queue length, head-of-line delay, arrival rate, reliability-class
indicator, duplication flag, one-hot protocol state, and protocol timer;
(ii) for the serving satellite: visibility, $\tilde\gamma$, $\tilde\eta$,
$\tilde\epsilon$, and $\tilde\Phi$; and (iii) for each candidate: presence, a
prepared-target flag, $\tilde\gamma$, $\tilde\eta$, $\tilde\epsilon$, $\tilde\Phi$,
and the observed SINR trend $\tilde\gamma(t)-\tilde\gamma(t-1)$.
Agents do \emph{not} observe the remaining visibility time, the capacity
multipliers $m_\ell$, the event realizations, the latent link regimes, or the
states and actions of other UEs. The candidate list is ranked by observed
current SINR only, and the action mask does not check whether a candidate will
remain visible long enough to complete a handover: anticipating the satellite
geometry is left to the decision layer. The per-UE reliability-class indicator
is fixed to $0.99$ in the present experiments and should not be confused with
the system-level outage constraint $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{out}}=0.10$ introduced
in Section~\ref{sec:problem}.

\emph{Per-epoch control sequence.}
At decision epoch $t$, UE $i$ forms its local observation from delayed
telemetry, its current protocol state, and its locally experienced history;
in M4 and M5 it additionally receives the satellite congestion prices
computed at the end of the preceding epoch. Each UE then selects one masked
mobility action independently. The environment first resolves ongoing
protocol timers and switching outcomes, re-validates the selected actions
(actions that have become infeasible are replaced by \textsc{Stay}), and
jointly admits the simultaneous preparation requests. New packets arrive,
the fixed scheduler allocates the remaining satellite resources to the
serving and duplicated data legs, and packet delivery, service deficit, and
outage are realized. The resulting local outcomes are fed to the recurrent
memory for the next decision. Finally, the hidden regimes and disturbance
events advance, the links and telemetry are refreshed, and the satellite
coordinator updates its congestion price from the aggregate intended demand,
so that the new price affects only the next decentralized decision.
Coordination is therefore causal and one-epoch delayed rather than based on
instantaneous access to the actions of other UEs.

\subsection{Non-Stationarity: Hidden Regimes and Disturbance Events}
\label{sec:nonstat}

\emph{Hidden link regimes.} Every UE--satellite path $(i,\ell)$ follows a
hidden semi-Markov regime $\textsc{Good}\to\textsc{Degraded}\to\textsc{Recovering}\to\textsc{Good}$.
A path enters \textsc{Degraded} with probability $p_{\mathrm{deg}}$ per epoch
(and with probability $p^{0}_{\mathrm{deg}}$ at the start of an episode),
stays there for $U\{8,\dots,24\}$ epochs with an extra loss
$Z_{i\ell}\sim\mathcal{U}[7,15]$\,dB, and then recovers over
$U\{4,\dots,10\}$ epochs during which $Z_{i\ell}$ decays linearly to zero.
These regimes persist over many epochs and are observable only through
their delayed, noisy effect on $\tilde\gamma$, which is what makes memory useful.

\emph{Disturbance events.} Each training and nominal-evaluation episode
contains five events whose start, duration, severity, affected satellite,
and affected UE subset are sampled from the ranges in Table~\ref{tab:params}:
two blockages (extra loss $B_{i\ell}$ on the selected UE--satellite links),
two capacity drops (multiplier $m_\ell<1$ on one satellite), and one traffic
burst ($\kappa_i>1$ on a subset of UEs). For the \emph{compound-stress} evaluation, the randomized training-event
schedule is replaced by a deterministic overlapping disturbance pattern: a
traffic burst ($\kappa=2.5$ on 50\% of the UEs, $t\in[60,120)$), a capacity
drop ($m_2=0.45$, $t\in[80,130)$), and an $18$-dB blockage affecting all
links of 25\% of the UEs during $t\in[100,125)$. The traffic-burst and
blockage severities lie outside their training ranges, and the blockage
affects all satellites of a UE instead of a single one, whereas the
capacity-drop magnitude equals the most severe value of its training range.
More importantly, the three disturbances overlap in time, creating a
compound operating condition not encountered during training.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Per-Epoch Costs}

Let $\Nset^{\mathrm{act}}(t)$ be the set of active UEs. We define the
service, control, and reliability costs of epoch $t$ as
\begin{align}
C_{\mathrm{def}}(t) &= \frac{1}{|\Nset^{\mathrm{act}}(t)|}\sum_{i\in\Nset^{\mathrm{act}}(t)}\delta_i(t),\\
C_{\mathrm{dup}}(t) &= \frac{B^{\mathrm{dup}}(t)}{B^{\mathrm{use}}(t)+B^{\mathrm{dup}}(t)},\\
C_{\mathrm{prep}}(t) &= \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{prep}}}{L}\sum_{\ell\in\Lset}n^{\mathrm{res}}_\ell(t),\\
C_{\mathrm{HO}}(t) &= \frac{1}{|\Nset^{\mathrm{act}}(t)|}\sum_{i\in\Nset^{\mathrm{act}}(t)}\ind\{a_i(t)=\textsc{Commit}\},\\
C_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(t) &= C_{\mathrm{dup}}(t)+\eta_{\mathrm{prep}}C_{\mathrm{prep}}(t)+\eta_{\mathrm{HO}}C_{\mathrm{HO}}(t),\\
C_{\mathrm{out}}(t) &= \frac{1}{|\Nset^{\mathrm{act}}(t)|}\sum_{i\in\Nset^{\mathrm{act}}(t)}o_i(t),
\end{align}
where $B^{\mathrm{use}}(t)$ denotes the volume of the original PDCP packet
stream scheduled for transmission during epoch $t$, before packet-delivery
errors are realized, and $B^{\mathrm{dup}}(t)$ denotes the additional
transmission volume generated by sending a copy of that stream over the
secondary leg. Hence, $C_{\mathrm{dup}}$ measures transmission redundancy rather than
successfully delivered traffic. $C_{\mathrm{ctrl}}$ is a resource- and
mobility-control-cost proxy comprising duplicated transmissions, target
preparation reservations, and handover executions. It does not explicitly
charge the number of preparation signalling messages; this distinction is
revisited in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Constrained Decentralized POMDP}

The problem is a cooperative, constrained decentralized partially observable
Markov decision process (Dec-POMDP)
$\langle\Nset,\mathcal{S},\{\Aset\},\mathcal{P},r,\{\Omega\},\mathcal{O},\gamma\rangle$.
The state $s(t)\in\mathcal{S}$ comprises the satellite geometry, channel and
shadowing states, hidden regimes, event realizations, queues, and protocol
states of all UEs; $\mathcal{P}$ is given by
Section~\ref{sec:system}; and each agent receives the local observation
$\mathbf{o}_i(t)\sim\mathcal{O}(\cdot\mid s(t))$ of Section~\ref{sec:obs}
together with its action mask. All agents share the team reward
\begin{equation}
r(t) = -C_{\mathrm{def}}(t) - \lambda\,C_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(t).
\label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
Reliability is imposed as a constraint on the long-run outage ratio rather
than as a further reward weight:
\begin{equation}
\max_{\boldsymbol\pi}\ \E_{\boldsymbol\pi}\Big[\sum_{t}\gamma^t r(t)\Big]
\ \ \text{s.t.}\ \ \E_{\boldsymbol\pi}\big[C_{\mathrm{out}}(t)\big]\le\varepsilon_{\mathrm{out}},
\label{eq:cdecpomdp}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol\pi=(\pi_1,\dots,\pi_N)$ are decentralized policies
$\pi_i(a_i\mid\mathbf{o}_i^{\le t})$ that may depend on the local observation
history. The per-satellite capacity constraints are enforced by the scheduler
and the admission rule and need not be learned. Problem~\eqref{eq:cdecpomdp}
is solved through its Lagrangian relaxation
\begin{gather}
\max_{\boldsymbol\pi}\min_{\mu\ge0}\ \E_{\boldsymbol\pi}\Big[\sum_t\gamma^t r_\mu(t)\Big],\label{eq:lagrangian}\\
r_\mu(t)=r(t)-\mu\big(C_{\mathrm{out}}(t)-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{out}}\big).\notag
\end{gather}

\emph{Why the problem is hard.} Three features make \eqref{eq:cdecpomdp}
difficult for conventional CHO rules and for memoryless learning:
(i) \emph{partial observability} with delayed, noisy telemetry and hidden,
persistent link regimes; (ii) \emph{delayed consequences}: a preparation
blocks resources and control slots before any benefit appears, the dwell guard
restricts future switches, and a target chosen now may set before the switch
completes; and (iii) \emph{coupling}: UEs compete for preparation slots,
reservations, and data capacity, and congestion lowers the handover success
probability \eqref{eq:hosuccess}.

\section{Proposed Capability-Structured Agentic Framework}
\label{sec:method}
% =====================================================================

We operationalize agentic control through a structured set of
capabilities rather than treating agentic AI as synonymous with an
LLM-based controller. The architecture combines a learned decentralized
policy with lightweight domain-specific mechanisms that exploit the structure
of LEO mobility, following the four capabilities of agentic AI.
\emph{Perception} is provided by masked, telemetry-based local observation and
per-UE autonomous action selection; \emph{memory} by a recurrent state that
infers persistent hidden conditions from delayed feedback; \emph{reasoning} by
a protocol-aware ephemeris lookahead that anticipates the delayed
consequences of preparation and switching; and \emph{coordination} by a
low-dimensional satellite congestion price that summarizes shared-resource
pressure without exchanging UE actions. Finally, a training-time reliability
multiplier provides explicit \emph{goal regulation}.

These capabilities are introduced incrementally to form the M1--M5 model
family in Table~\ref{tab:models}; Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the resulting
agent. The resulting ladder is an architectural
decomposition used for capability-structured evaluation, not an assumption
that performance must improve monotonically from M1 to M5. Each variant is
trained independently in the same environment with the same base reward,
except for the reliability Lagrangian used by M5. No language model is
required, and online execution remains numerical and lightweight: actions
are produced locally by each UE, while satellites broadcast only the scalar
congestion prices required by M4 and M5.

\subsection{Perception and Core Decision Making (M1)}

All UEs share one stochastic actor $\pi_\theta$ and are trained with
centralized training and decentralized execution (CTDE) using multi-agent
proximal policy optimization (MAPPO)~\cite{yu2022mappo}. The actor maps its
input $\mathbf{z}_i(t)$ to logits over $\Aset$, and invalid actions are removed
by a masked softmax,
\begin{equation}
\pi_\theta(a\mid\mathbf{z}_i,\mathbf{m}_i)=\frac{m_{i,a}\exp\big(\ell_\theta(\mathbf{z}_i)_a\big)}{\sum_{a'}m_{i,a'}\exp\big(\ell_\theta(\mathbf{z}_i)_{a'}\big)}.
\end{equation}
Perception in M1 is the masked, telemetry-based observation
$\mathbf{z}_i=\mathbf{o}_i$. The centralized critic $V_\psi(\mathbf{s}^{c})$ is
used only during training. Its input $\mathbf{s}^{c}(t)$ concatenates the
observations of all UEs with the true per-satellite utilization and pressure
$\Phi_\ell$, and, when available, the satellite prices and the dual variable.

\subsection{Memory: Inferring Hidden Regimes (M2)}

Because link regimes are hidden and telemetry is delayed, the current
observation is not a sufficient statistic. M2 equips every agent with a gated
recurrent unit (GRU) that summarizes its interaction history,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_i(t)=\mathrm{GRU}_\theta\big(\mathbf{h}_i(t-1),\ [\mathbf{o}_i(t),\ \mathbf{e}(a_i(t-1)),\ \boldsymbol\zeta_i(t-1)]\big),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{e}(\cdot)$ is a one-hot encoding and
$\boldsymbol\zeta_i\in\mathbb{R}^6$ is the agent's own \emph{experienced
outcome}: the last handover result ($+1$ success, $-1$ failure, $0$ none), its
outage indicator $o_i$, its deficit $\delta_i$, the duplication gain
$[g_i-g_i^{\mathrm{prim}}]^+/R_i$, its resource-usage indicator, and the
experienced congestion $\Phi/(1+\Phi)$ of its serving or target satellite.
The actor input becomes $\mathbf{z}_i=[\mathbf{o}_i,\mathbf{h}_i]$. Memory
thus supports long-horizon adaptation to non-stationary link conditions using
only locally available feedback. The GRU is trained end-to-end with truncated
backpropagation through time.

\subsection{Reasoning: Protocol-Aware Ephemeris Lookahead (M3)}

The mask does not prevent choosing a target that is about to set, and the
benefit of a preparation appears only several epochs later. M3 therefore adds
an analytical planner that exploits the one piece of information that is
deterministic in LEO systems: the satellite ephemeris. For every feasible
first action $a$, the planner evaluates a small library $\Pi_i(a)$ of
protocol-consistent plans of length $H$ (e.g., $\textsc{Prep}_k$--wait--$\textsc{Commit}$;
$\textsc{Prep}_k$--wait--$\textsc{DupOn}$--$\textsc{Commit}$; $\textsc{Stay}$),
padded with $\textsc{Stay}$, by rolling the UE's own protocol state machine
forward. For tractability, this hypothetical rollout propagates protocol
timers deterministically. Once a hypothetical \textsc{Commit} satisfies the
dwell and predicted-visibility conditions, completion of the corresponding
switch is treated as successful in the lookahead. Consequently, the stochastic
handover-success model in \eqref{eq:hosuccess} is realized by the environment
but is not explicitly sampled or averaged inside the analytical planner.

The future SINR of link $(i,\ell)$ is extrapolated from the
\emph{observed} SINR and the ephemeris-predicted range,
\begin{equation}
\hat\gamma^{\mathrm{dB}}_{i\ell}(t+\tau)=\tilde\gamma^{\mathrm{dB}}_{i\ell}(t)-20\log_{10}\frac{d_{i\ell}(t+\tau)}{d_{i\ell}(t)},
\end{equation}
and set to zero once $\epsilon_{i\ell}(t+\tau)<\epsilon_{\min}$. The predicted
leg rate uses the nominal capacity $a^0$ scaled by the observed load
$\tilde\Phi_{i\ell}$, from which the planner obtains the predicted deficit
$\hat\delta_i(t+\tau)$, including the duplication diversity of \eqref{eq:dup}.
The cost of a first action is the best discounted plan cost,
\begin{multline}
J_i(a)=\min_{\varsigma\in\Pi_i(a)}\sum_{\tau=0}^{H-1}\xi^\tau\Big(\hat\delta_i(t+\tau\mid\varsigma)\\
+\ind_{\mathrm{coord}}\,\mathbf{y}(t)^{\mathsf T}\big[\hat{\mathbf{u}}_i(t+\tau\mid\varsigma)-\hat{\mathbf{u}}_i^{\textsc{Stay}}(t+\tau)\big]^+\Big),
\label{eq:plancost}
\end{multline}
where $\ind_{\mathrm{coord}}=1$ for M4 and M5 and is zero otherwise.
Here, $\mathbf{y}(t)=[y_1(t),\ldots,y_L(t)]^{\mathsf T}$ denotes the
\emph{raw} satellite-price vector maintained by the coordinator. Only entries
corresponding to the UE's serving and hypothetical target satellites contribute
to the inner product.

The vector $\hat{\mathbf{u}}_i$ represents the planner's predicted
per-satellite resource footprint of UE $i$. In the present implementation it
contains the predicted serving-leg resource fraction, the secondary-leg
resource fraction while duplicating, and the data-plane preparation reservation
$\rho_{\mathrm{prep}}$ while a target reservation is active. The positive-part
difference with $\hat{\mathbf{u}}_i^{\textsc{Stay}}$ therefore prices only the
incremental resource footprint relative to remaining with the current serving
satellite.

Importantly, $\hat{\mathbf{u}}_i$ is not identical to the aggregate intended
demand $U_\ell$ used by the satellite coordinator (Section~\ref{sec:coord}).
The control-plane preparation-contention term $n_\ell^{\mathrm{req}}/M$ is
included in $U_\ell$ when the satellite price is updated, but is not
explicitly inserted into the single-UE hypothetical demand vector. Its effect
therefore reaches the reasoner indirectly through the broadcast congestion
price.

The reasoner deliberately ranks plans primarily by predicted service
continuity rather than reproducing the complete MAPPO training reward. In M3,
the second term of \eqref{eq:plancost} is absent, so resource and
mobility-control costs are learned through the team reward rather than
hard-coded into the planner. M4 and M5 add the marginal raw-price term, allowing
the same geometry-aware lookahead to account for recently observed shared
resource pressure.

Let $J_i^{\min}$ and $J_i^{\max}$ be the minimum and maximum of $J_i(a)$ over
the feasible actions. The reasoning feature is
\begin{equation}
\phi_{i,a}=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{J_i(a)-J_i^{\min}}{J_i^{\max}-J_i^{\min}+\epsilon_\phi}, & J_i^{\max}-J_i^{\min}>\epsilon_\phi,\\[6pt]
0, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:reasoning_feature}
\end{equation}
for feasible actions, clipped to $[0,1]$, and $\phi_{i,a}=1$ for masked
actions. The vector $\boldsymbol\phi_i\in[0,1]^{|\Aset|}$ is appended to the actor input:
$\mathbf{z}_i=[\mathbf{o}_i,\mathbf{h}_i,\boldsymbol\phi_i]$. The planner is
\emph{advisory}: the actor learns how much to trust it. It has no access to
future events, hidden regimes, true capacity multipliers, or the states of
other UEs.

\subsection{Coordination: Congestion Prices (M4)}
\label{sec:coord}

UEs compete for preparation slots, reservations, and data capacity, but
exchanging intentions between UEs is impractical. M4 coordinates them through
a lightweight price signal maintained by each satellite. Satellite $\ell$
measures its aggregate intended demand
\begin{equation}
U_\ell(t)=\sum_{i}f^{\mathrm{serv}}_{i\ell}(t)+\frac{n^{\mathrm{req}}_\ell(t)}{M}+\sum_i f^{\mathrm{dup}}_{i\ell}(t),
\end{equation}
i.e., the serving demand, the preparation requests normalized by the number of
control slots, and the duplication demand, and updates its price by
projected subgradient ascent,
\begin{equation}
y_\ell(t+1)=\Big[y_\ell(t)+\alpha_y\big(U_\ell(t)-1\big)\Big]_0^{y_{\max}}.
\label{eq:price}
\end{equation}
Prices rise while intended demand exceeds the normalized resource level and
decay otherwise; they are reset at the start of each episode and are not
learned. The price produced at the end of epoch $t$ is used only for the next
decentralized decision, so coordination is causal and one-epoch delayed.

For the actor, UE $i$ receives the normalized local price feature
\begin{equation}
\bar{\mathbf{y}}_i(t)
=
\frac{
[y_{p_i}(t),y_{c_{i1}}(t),\ldots,y_{c_{iK}}(t)]
}{y_{\max}}.
\end{equation}
In contrast, the analytical lookahead in \eqref{eq:plancost} uses the raw
prices $y_\ell(t)$ rather than their normalized actor representation. This
distinction reflects the implementation: normalization is used to provide a
bounded neural-network input, whereas the raw price directly weights the
planner's incremental resource demand.

The coordinator never allocates actions: decisions remain decentralized, and
the signalling required for coordination is one scalar price per satellite and
control epoch. The price should therefore be interpreted as compressed
coordination context rather than a centralized allocation command. It reveals
neither individual UE states, actions, queues, nor future events, and conveys
only the recent aggregate resource pressure seen by each satellite.

\subsection{Goal Regulation: Primal--Dual Reliability Control (M5)}

M5 addresses the Lagrangian problem~\eqref{eq:lagrangian}. The dual update uses
the realized outage fraction over all active-UE opportunities in one rollout.
For rollout index set $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{roll}}$, the implementation
computes
\begin{equation}
\bar C^{\mathrm{roll}}_{\mathrm{out}}
=
\frac{
\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{roll}}}
\sum_{i\in\Nset^{\mathrm{act}}(t)} o_i(t)
}{
\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{roll}}}
|\Nset^{\mathrm{act}}(t)|
}.
\end{equation}
After every rollout of $T_{\mathrm{roll}}$ epochs, the multiplier is updated
by the projected step
\begin{equation}
\mu\leftarrow\Big[\mu+\alpha_\mu\big(\bar C^{\mathrm{roll}}_{\mathrm{out}}-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{out}}\big)\Big]_0^{\mu_{\max}},
\label{eq:dual}
\end{equation}
and the policy is trained on $r_\mu$. The multiplier is held fixed within
each rollout, updated between policy iterations, and frozen after training.
In our implementation, $\mu$ does not modify the ranking of the analytical
lookahead; reliability enters the learned decision through the Lagrangian
reward, the recurrently observed outcomes, and the normalized feature
$\mu/\mu_{\max}$ supplied to the actor and the critic. Consequently, $\mu=0$
makes the \emph{instantaneous training reward} identical to the M4 base
reward, but a trained M5 network remains a separately optimized policy and is
not identical to M4.

\subsection{Training Procedure and Complexity}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:train} summarizes training. The shared actor and the
centralized critic are optimized with the clipped PPO
objective~\cite{schulman2017ppo} and generalized advantage
estimation~\cite{schulman2016gae} on the team reward ($r$ or $r_\mu$), with
early stopping at a target KL divergence and linearly annealed learning rates.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Training of the capability-structured agents}
\label{alg:train}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State Initialize $\theta$ (actor, GRU), $\psi$ (critic), $\mu\gets0$
\For{iteration $=1,\dots,I$}
  \For{$t=1,\dots,T_{\mathrm{roll}}$}
    \State Each UE forms $\mathbf{o}_i(t)$ and mask $\mathbf{m}_i(t)$ \Comment{perception}
    \State $\mathbf{h}_i(t)\gets\mathrm{GRU}_\theta(\cdot)$ \Comment{M2+}
    \State $\boldsymbol\phi_i(t)\gets$ lookahead \eqref{eq:plancost} with prices $\mathbf{y}(t)$ \Comment{M3+}
    \State $a_i(t)\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid\mathbf{z}_i(t),\mathbf{m}_i(t))$ for all $i$ in parallel
    \State Environment step; observe $r(t)$, $C_{\mathrm{out}}(t)$, outcomes $\boldsymbol\zeta_i(t)$
    \State Satellites update prices by \eqref{eq:price} \Comment{M4+}
  \EndFor
  \State Compute $r_\mu$ with the current $\mu$ \Comment{M5}
  \State Update $\theta,\psi$ with PPO, GAE, and TBPTT
  \State Update $\mu$ by \eqref{eq:dual} \Comment{M5}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\emph{Execution complexity.} In each control epoch, a UE performs one
recurrent-state update and one actor forward pass. For M3--M5, the analytical
reasoner evaluates at most $\max\{2K+1,4\}$ protocol-consistent plans, each
over a horizon of $H$ epochs. Its per-agent planning complexity is therefore
$\mathcal{O}(KH)$ and does not grow with the total number of UEs. Across all
$N$ agents, the aggregate reasoning work is $\mathcal{O}(NKH)$, but the $N$
per-UE computations are mutually independent and can be executed in
parallel. Coordination requires only one congestion price per satellite and
epoch, and the M5 reliability multiplier introduces no execution-time
inter-UE signalling.
Measured on a single CPU thread of a desktop processor with an unoptimized
Python/NumPy implementation, the mean actor inference time per epoch for all
$N=12$ UEs is $0.24$--$0.44$\,ms, and the analytical reasoner adds
$19.6$--$21.3$\,ms (95th percentile at most $26.3$\,ms). The total decision
latency of M3--M5 is thus at most $21.7$\,ms on average ($26.8$\,ms at the 95th
percentile), i.e., about $1.8$\,ms per UE, which is more than an order of
magnitude below the $\Delta=1$\,s control epoch.

\section{Simulation Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Training}
All variants use the environment and parameters of Table~\ref{tab:params}.
Each variant is trained for $I=2000$ iterations with five independent
training seeds $\{7,8,9,10,11\}$; the seed determines both the network
initialization and the training-environment realization. The five M1--M5
variants therefore comprise $25$ independently trained policies. Training
episodes contain the randomized disturbance schedule of
Section~\ref{sec:nonstat}; the compound-stress pattern is never used for
training.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
Every trained policy is evaluated on five held-out evaluation seeds with
$40$ episodes each, under two conditions: \emph{nominal} (randomized events
drawn as in training) and \emph{compound stress}
(Section~\ref{sec:nonstat}). The episode streams of different evaluation
seeds are disjoint, and all methods are evaluated on identical episode
streams, which yields paired comparisons. Actions are sampled from the
stochastic policies; congestion prices evolve online and are reset at the
start of each episode, and the multiplier of M5 is frozen at its trained
value. For a learned variant, each training seed is first averaged over the
five evaluation seeds, and the mean and the Student-$t$ $95\%$ confidence
interval (CI) are computed across the five training seeds, so the CI
reflects training variability. Rule-based baselines have no training seed;
their CI is computed across the evaluation seeds and mainly reflects the
difficulty of the individual scenarios. Besides the realized outage ratio,
we report the service deficit, the number of handover executions per epoch
(summed over the $N$ UEs), the ping-pong ratio (ping-pongs per successful
handover), the resource cost $C_{\mathrm{dup}}+\eta_{\mathrm{prep}}C_{\mathrm{prep}}+\eta_{\mathrm{HO}}C_{\mathrm{HO}}$,
and the \emph{common reward} $r=-C_{\mathrm{def}}-\lambda C_{\mathrm{ctrl}}$
of~\eqref{eq:reward}. The common reward is used for all methods, including
M5, whose training reward additionally contains the Lagrangian term.
% [확인] resource cost = C_ctrl (control cost)입니다. 표의 "Resource"와 common reward의 C_ctrl가 같은 양입니다.

\subsection{Baselines}
All baselines observe the same delayed and noisy telemetry and are subject
to the same action masks and admission rules as the learned agents.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{CHO (A3/TTT)}: a 3GPP-inspired conditional handover. The best
observed candidate is prepared when it exceeds the serving satellite by
$O_{\mathrm{prep}}=1$\,dB; the prepared target is executed when it exceeds
the serving satellite by $O_{\mathrm{exec}}$ for $T_{\mathrm{TTT}}$
consecutive epochs, and it is released after it falls below the serving
satellite by $1$\,dB for three epochs. $O_{\mathrm{exec}}\in\{2,3,4\}$\,dB
and $T_{\mathrm{TTT}}\in\{1,2,3\}$ epochs were tuned for the common reward
on three validation seeds whose episodes are disjoint from all evaluation
episodes, and then frozen ($O_{\mathrm{exec}}=2$\,dB, $T_{\mathrm{TTT}}=1$).
The selected offset is the smallest value of the grid, and $T_{\mathrm{TTT}}=1$
is the smallest feasible value at $\Delta=1$\,s.
\item \emph{CHO (A3/TTT)+Dup}: the tuned CHO rule, additionally activating
duplication while a target is prepared and the serving SINR is below a
threshold tuned in the same way ($6$\,dB, selected from $\{2,4,6\}$\,dB).
\item \emph{Max-SINR}: prepares the best observed candidate when it exceeds
the serving satellite by $1$\,dB and executes as soon as allowed, without
duplication.
\item \emph{Always-Dup}: always prepares the best observed candidate,
activates duplication, and executes.
\item \emph{Threshold-Dup}: prepares the best candidate when the serving SINR
falls below $4$\,dB, activates duplication, and executes when the target
exceeds the serving satellite by $1$\,dB.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Comparison with Rule-Based Mobility Control}
Table~\ref{tab:main} and Fig.~\ref{fig:outage} show that every learned
variant achieves a much lower outage ratio than all rule-based schemes. In
the nominal condition, the learned variants attain $0.131$--$0.149$, whereas
the baselines attain $0.322$--$0.429$. Relative to the tuned CHO baseline
(Fig.~\ref{fig:m5cho}), M5 reduces the outage ratio by $60\%$ ($0.137$ vs.\
$0.345$), the service deficit by $52\%$, the ping-pong ratio by $79\%$, the
number of failed handovers by $67\%$, and deadline violations and queue
overflows by more than $50\%$; under compound stress, the outage reduction is $46\%$.
The advantage is not an artefact of averaging: all $125$ policy--scenario
pairs ($25$ trained policies $\times$ $5$ evaluation seeds) have a lower
outage ratio than CHO in the same scenario, in both conditions. The wide CIs
of the baselines stem from differences in scenario difficulty; in paired
terms, the ratio between the outage of M5 and that of CHO stays within
$0.38$--$0.42$ (nominal) and $0.52$--$0.56$ (stress) across the evaluation
seeds. The price of this reliability is a moderately higher resource cost:
CHO almost never duplicates and thus spends $2.6\times10^{-3}$ versus
$4.3\times10^{-3}$ for M5. Adding a duplication trigger to CHO barely changes
its behavior (duplication is active in $0.06\%$ of the time), which indicates
that reliable use of duplication requires anticipating when a second leg is
worth its cost, rather than a fixed SINR threshold.

\subsection{Capability-Structured Comparison of M1--M5}
Among the learned variants, reliability is similar, and the capabilities
mainly change \emph{how} the agents achieve it (Fig.~\ref{fig:capability}
and Table~\ref{tab:main}). The memoryless M1 attains the lowest outage ratio
($0.131\pm0.008$) and the best common reward, but it does so with the most
mobility actions. \emph{Memory} (M2) halves the ping-pong ratio ($-49\%$) and
reduces handover executions by $21\%$ relative to M1, at the price of a
$1.3$ percentage-point (pp) higher outage ratio, which is consistent with the
agent learning to ride out transient degradations instead of reacting to
every telemetry fluctuation. \emph{Reasoning} (M3) yields no measurable gain
over M2 (outage $+0.5$\,pp, handover executions $+10\%$), although it has the
smallest variance of the outage ratio across training seeds. Adding
\emph{coordination} alone (M3$\to$M4) gives mixed results: the outage ratio
decreases slightly, but handover executions and resource cost increase, and
the variance across training seeds grows.

The largest change occurs when \emph{goal regulation} is added (M4$\to$M5):
handover executions decrease by $35\%$, the ping-pong ratio by $53\%$, the
resource cost by $44\%$, and failed handovers by $32\%$, while the outage
ratio also decreases slightly. At the same time, the variability across
independently trained policies shrinks markedly: the CI half-width of the
handover executions drops from $0.115$ to $0.046$ and that of the ping-pong
ratio from $0.039$ to $0.007$. As a result, the full stack operates at a
distinct point of the reliability--mobility trade-off (Fig.~\ref{fig:capability}):
compared with M1, M5 performs $39\%$ fewer handover executions, has a $70\%$
lower ping-pong ratio, a $21\%$ lower resource cost, and $19\%$ fewer failed
handovers, for a $0.6$\,pp higher outage ratio whose CI overlaps that of M1.
The same pattern holds under compound stress ($-34\%$, $-66\%$, and $-16\%$
for handover executions, ping-pong ratio, and resource cost, respectively,
at $+0.5$\,pp outage). We emphasize that the M1--M5 ladder is not monotone:
the capabilities interact, and their benefit materializes in combination.

\subsection{Is the Coordination Signal Used?}
Because the variants are trained independently, the M3$\to$M4 comparison
alone cannot reveal whether the agents rely on the congestion price. We
therefore evaluate each trained M4 and M5 policy a second time on the same
episodes with the price forced to zero, which isolates the contribution of the
signal within a fixed policy (Table~\ref{tab:ablation} and
Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation}). Removing the price increases the handover
executions by $18$--$24\%$, the ping-pong ratio by $22$--$35\%$, and the
resource cost by $20$--$26\%$, in \emph{every one} of the ten policies and in
both conditions. The outage ratio increases in all ten policies in the nominal
condition ($+5$--$7\%$) and in eight of ten under stress, where the effect is
small and its CI includes zero. The price is thus an operative input that
all ten price-enabled M4/M5 policies have learned to exploit, mainly to
suppress unnecessary mobility actions and redundant transmissions. A detailed single-policy
diagnostic (training seed $7$, $5\times200$ episodes under compound stress)
shows the underlying mechanism: with the price, the agent still prepares
candidates but commits more selectively, i.e., the fraction of preparations
that end in a switch decreases from $12.8\%$ to $9.4\%$ and more preparations
are released without committing. Two caveats apply. First, zeroing the price
at evaluation is a distribution shift for a policy trained with it, so the
effect measures reliance on the signal rather than the value of training with
it. Second, as noted above, adding coordination alone does not guarantee a
better operating point; its benefit is consolidated when combined with goal
regulation in M5.

\subsection{How Do the Agents Use Conditional Handover?}
Fig.~\ref{fig:behavior} and Table~\ref{tab:extended} reveal that the learned
agents use the CHO procedure in a qualitatively different way from the rule.
The tuned CHO rule stays in $87.8\%$ of its decisions and, once it prepares a
target, executes the switch in $73\%$ of the cases; it holds a preparation for
$5.3$ epochs on average, and $49\%$ of its preparation requests are rejected
because many UEs request the same satellite at the same time. The learned
agents instead treat a preparation as a short-lived \emph{option}. M5 issues a
preparation in $38\%$ of its decisions and releases most of them, so that only
$6.3\%$ of the preparations end in a switch; a preparation is held for only
$1.3$ epochs, i.e., little longer than the preparation timer. This
prepare-broadly, commit-selectively behavior is progressively reinforced
along the capability ladder (prepare-to-commit ratio of $18.9\%$ for M1,
$9.7$--$13.5\%$ for M2--M4, and $6.3\%$ for M5) and is accompanied by fewer
admission conflicts: the preparation rejection rate falls from $35\%$ (M1) to
$20\%$ (M5), compared with $49\%$ for CHO, which is consistent with the agents
learning to avoid contended targets. PDCP duplication, in contrast, is used
very sparingly by all learned variants (active $0.2$--$0.6\%$ of the time),
whereas activating it indiscriminately (Always-Dup, $22.8\%$) degrades every
metric; under the present cost weights, the agents learn that the resource
cost of a second leg is rarely justified. The prepare-and-release behavior
comes at a price in control-plane signalling: M5 issues $4.6$ preparation
requests per epoch, compared with $1.0$ for CHO. Since the reward charges the
reserved resources but not the number of signalling messages, this overhead
is not penalized during training and is discussed as a limitation in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Service, Satellite Load, and Signalling}
The reliability advantage translates directly into delivered traffic
(Table~\ref{tab:extended}). The learned variants
deliver $92.1$--$93.1\%$ of the arriving packets in the nominal condition and
$85.3$--$86.2\%$ under compound stress, compared with $84.7\%$ and $78.6\%$ for
CHO; deadline violations and queue overflows are roughly halved
($2.1$--$2.4\%$ vs.\ $4.7\%$ and $4.5$--$5.2\%$ vs.\ $10.3\%$, respectively).
A failed switch is also about half as likely per execution ($4.7$--$6.2\%$
vs.\ $11.6\%$). The learned agents carry more traffic---the mean satellite
utilization is about $50\%$ versus $36\%$ for CHO---and nevertheless lower the mean of the per-epoch peak requested pressure
from $1.35$ to $1.15$--$1.20$ and reduce the number of overloaded satellites, i.e., they
spread the load more evenly across the constellation slice. Under compound
stress, the peak-pressure advantage persists ($1.23$--$1.29$ vs.\ $1.43$),
whereas the number of overloaded satellites becomes similar to CHO because the
disturbances force overload regardless of the association policy.

\subsection{Robustness to Compound Stress}
Under the compound-stress pattern, whose traffic-burst and blockage
severities lie outside the training range, all learned variants remain far
more reliable than the rule-based schemes (Table~\ref{tab:main}), and the
efficiency advantages of M5 are preserved. However, the outage ratio of the
learned variants increases by $5.9$--$6.4$\,pp, compared with $2.5$\,pp for CHO
and $3.0$\,pp for Max-SINR. The learned policies thus retain a large absolute
advantage but are more sensitive to out-of-distribution disturbances than the
rules, and none of the capabilities provides a specific robustness gain.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
% =====================================================================

\emph{Reliability target.} Although M5 regulates the outage pressure during
training, the outage ratio on the held-out scenarios ($0.137$) remains above
the target $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{out}}=0.10$ for all variants. In addition,
changing the frozen multiplier at evaluation hardly changes the behavior of
M5. The dual controller should therefore be understood as a training-time
regulator rather than as a goal-conditioned policy that adapts its operating
point on demand; enforcing the constraint on unseen scenarios and training
with randomized multipliers are open problems.

\emph{Reasoning.} The ephemeris-based reasoner did not improve performance in
our setting. Several implementation choices may contribute to this result.
First, the planner extrapolates delayed and noisy radio telemetry. Second, its
hypothetical protocol rollout treats a feasible handover completion
deterministically, whereas the actual environment resolves the switch
stochastically according to \eqref{eq:hosuccess}. Third, the single-UE
hypothetical resource vector accounts for the data-plane preparation
reservation but not the explicit control-slot contention term
$n_\ell^{\mathrm{req}}/M$; the latter reaches the planner only indirectly
through the congestion price. Finally, the recurrent memory may already capture
part of the temporal structure that the lookahead attempts to expose. These
observations suggest that using the planner as an action prior, incorporating an
expected handover-success model, or explicitly representing preparation
contention are natural directions for future study.

\emph{Mechanism of goal regulation.} The consistent efficiency gain and the
variance reduction from M4 to M5 are empirical observations; why the
Lagrangian reward stabilizes the learned operating point is not yet
understood and deserves further study.

\emph{Signalling and duplication.} The reward charges reserved preparation
resources but not the number of preparation messages. The learned
prepare-and-release behavior therefore generates $3.5$--$4.7$ times more
preparation requests than the CHO rule, and a message-level signalling cost
should be added to confirm that the observed advantages persist when control
signalling is explicitly priced. Moreover, the learned agents activate PDCP
duplication only rarely under the present cost weights; the regimes in which
duplication becomes worthwhile (e.g., stricter reliability classes or lower
duplication cost) remain to be characterized.

\emph{Model scope.} The study uses a stylized constellation slice with six
satellites and twelve UEs, a fixed interference floor, a system-level
protocol abstraction, and a fixed scheduler. Scalability to larger
constellations and UE populations, explicit interference, and message-level
protocol effects remain to be evaluated. Finally, the statistics rely on five
independently trained policies per variant.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% =====================================================================

We formulated the joint control of CHO preparation, PDCP duplication, and
primary-satellite switching in a shared-capacity LEO network as a constrained
Dec-POMDP and proposed a capability-structured agentic framework that
instantiates perception, memory, reasoning, and coordination through
lightweight, numerical mechanisms matched to LEO mobility, together with a
training-time goal regulator. Across $25$ independently trained policies,
every learned policy--scenario pair achieved lower outage than the tuned
3GPP-inspired CHO rule. For M5, the mean outage reduction was $60\%$
under nominal conditions and $46\%$ under compound stress. The full
capability stack achieved reliability close to that of the simplest
learned policy while reducing handover executions by $39\%$ and the
ping-pong ratio by $70\%$ under nominal conditions, with markedly smaller
variability across trained policies, and within-policy ablations showed
that all ten price-enabled M4/M5 policies exploit the congestion price.
A behavioral analysis showed that the agents use CHO preparations as
short-lived options and commit only a small fraction of them, which reduces
admission conflicts, raises the packet delivery ratio by about eight
percentage points relative to the rule, and lowers the mean per-epoch peak requested pressure.
The results also delimit the approach: the reasoning module did not help,
the reliability target was not met on unseen scenarios, the learned policies
were more sensitive to compound disturbances than the rules, and their
prepare-and-release behavior increases control-plane signalling, which the
present reward does not price. These findings
indicate that agentic capabilities are most effective in combination, and
they point to goal-conditioned regulation and planner-guided action selection
as the most promising directions.
